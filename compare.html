Compassionism beside other designs

What happens to poverty, cost and work when the same 500 simulated adults live under Compassionism, under five other ways of helping people, and under today's US safety net. This page reads saved results; it runs nothing, and it says which engine version each figure comes from.

Read this first. This is an exploratory model, not a forecast, and it has not been reviewed by an independent economist. The figures below come from two different versions of the engine and are never combined: Part 1 is from engine v4.22 (the last version that ran all six designs beside each other), Part 3 is from engine v5.5 and v5.6 (the Health Study and release data, which put today's safety net in every run). The model measures poverty, cost and work. It does not measure the cultural and creative effects Compassionism aspires to, so a design can look better or worse here than it would in the world.

How Compassionism compares with other whole systems. This page compares anti-poverty designs inside one simulation. For how Compassionism compares with other comprehensive systems (for example, systems of economics, governance and social organisation) on a wider set of values, see the Normative Evaluation.

Part 1. Six designs on the same simulated adults (engine v4.22)

Each design is paid for by the same flat contribution on wages, set each year to cover its cost, and adults adjust how much they work by the same rules under every design (earnings fall by 16 cents per unconditional dollar, the rate measured in the largest US study of unconditional cash). Poverty here is measured against a living-wage basket and the deepest shortfalls are weighted most (FGT2). Negative is less poverty. Brackets are 95% intervals across paired seeds. Today's safety net is not in these runs; Part 3 adds it.

Part 2. Compassionism Core and Compassionism with its untested mechanisms

The like-for-like row, Compassionism Core, is the engine's Compassionism with the two untested mechanisms switched off: the wage raise for each octave held, and slower inflation in proportion to PTF and PTH membership. It can be set beside the other designs without giving Compassionism extras the others do not get. The row above it in the table, Compassionism, as coded, has both mechanisms on, which is closer to the Hub's picture of the design but rests on two assumptions that are still theoretical; they carry most of Compassionism's edge in these runs. The button specified on the Hub switches to the Hub's written conversion design (businesses convert the BU they accept, and expired BU go to projects that participants convert), which costs about two and a half to three times as much per adult. The model measures none of the cultural effects the framework holds make that cost worthwhile. Compassionism as designed differs from the Core in exactly these ways, so read the Core row for the like-for-like comparison and the others as the design's aim

Part 3. Compassionism against today's US safety net (engine v5.5 and v5.6)

Every gain should hold on all three measures. A design that lowers the first two while the third rises is not a confirmed gain. In the three rows above, poverty below the cost of living and below 30 days of basic living falls in every environment (except 30-days poverty in the Stress Test, where it rises), but too little wealth at year 20 rises in all three, so none of them is a confirmed gain on every measure. In the model that rise comes from prices rising while savings are not protected from inflation.

Limits

  • Part 1 predates the household, children, health and labour-market mechanisms and the safety net; it has not been re-run on the current engine. A like-for-like re-run of all six designs on engine v5.6, with the safety net in every arm, needs a new 500-run study on a Runpod pod and is the next step.
  • The comparison designs are simplified versions of real proposals; each is described, with sources, on the earlier-engine page.
  • Public costs of poverty (crime, homelessness services, emergency health care) are credited in the current release but were not in Part 1, so no design in Part 1 is credited with the savings from poverty it removes.
  • This page is kept out of search results until Duke approves it.
The math and code of this simulation were engineered by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, from the concepts in Duke Johnson's book Better To Best and his related vision for eradicating extreme poverty while enriching cultures and supporting human flourishing. The model has not yet been reviewed by an independent economist; the full code is open for anyone to check, and expert collaborators are welcome.
