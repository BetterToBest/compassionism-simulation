Compassionism beside other designs

What happens to poverty, cost and work when the same 500 simulated adults live under Compassionism, under five other ways of helping people, and under today's US safety net. This page reads saved results; it runs nothing, and it says which engine version each figure comes from.

Read this first. This is an exploratory model, not a forecast, and it has not been reviewed by an independent economist. The figures below come from two different versions of the engine and are never combined: Parts 1 and 2 are from the current engine (v5.5 and v5.6, with today's safety net in every run), and Part 3 is from the earlier engine v4.22, the last version that ran all six designs beside each other without a safety net. The model measures poverty, cost and work. It does not measure the cultural and creative effects Compassionism aspires to, so a design can look better or worse here than it would in the world.

How Compassionism compares with other whole systems. This page compares anti-poverty designs inside one simulation. For how Compassionism compares with other comprehensive systems (for example, systems of economics, governance and social organisation) on a wider set of values, see the Normative Evaluation.

Key points

  • Money-created financing (Part 1, Reference environment, 20 years). Against a world with no program, Compassionism (the like-for-like Core row) lowers poverty severity by 6.80; a UBI of the same cost lowers it by 7.85, and a negative income tax by 8.05. The asset endowment lowers it by 0.98, X-Cents by 1.96.
  • The price of that. When the program is paid for with newly created money, prices rise by 41.8 points a year with Compassionism, 62.4 with the UBI, 75.4 with the negative income tax and 15.1 with the asset endowment. A design that raises prices less is not automatically better for people.
  • How the program is paid for changes the answer. With a flat contribution on wages instead, prices do not move but earnings are taxed, and in the Reference environment the Compassionism Core row no longer lowers poverty severity (it rises by 4.03) while the UBI still lowers it (by 2.25).
  • Against today's safety net (Part 2, Reference). The share of adult-years below the cost of living falls from 49.3% to 19.6%, but the share with too little wealth at year 20 rises from 32.7% to 44.4%, so it is not a confirmed gain on every measure.

Model results, not forecasts. Every figure is explained in the part it comes from, in all three environments, with 95% ranges.

Part 1. Six designs on the same simulated adults, current engine (v5.6)

Claude’s reading of the design This is the latest comparison: the six designs on the current engine (500 simulated adults, the same 500 paired runs in each environment). Every arm, including no program, has today's safety net (SNAP with its work rule, the earned income tax credit and the child tax credit).

  • Equal cost. The UBI, the negative income tax and the asset endowment are each sized to cost the same as the Compassionism specification from the Research Hub. X-Cents and public grocery are shown at their own, much smaller, cost.
  • Two ways to pay. Under the Source pays the program is paid for with newly created money, so prices rise (in this model the rise can be very large) and wealth poverty rises under every cash design because savings lose value. Under a flat contribution on wages pays there is no price effect, but earnings are taxed. The answer depends on which rule is used, so both are shown.
  • Source pays, all three environments. At equal cost, Compassionism raises prices less than the UBI and the negative income tax but more than the asset endowment, and it lowers poverty severity less than the UBI and the negative income tax. X-Cents and public grocery cost far less and move poverty far less.
  • Wage contribution pays. The UBI lowers severity more than Compassionism in the Reference and Adverse environments, the asset endowment raises it sharply, and Compassionism and the UBI are roughly level in the Stress Test.

The gaps between designs are results of this model, not forecasts, and the model does not measure cultural or creative effects. The 40-year versions and the exact commands are in the results repository (BetterToBest/compassionism-sim-results, folder s171-cfc3322); the full tables there also show price levels.

How to read the rows. Compassionism Core is the like-for-like row: the Hub specification with the two untested mechanisms switched off, so it gets no extras the other designs do not. The other Compassionism rows show what changes if those mechanisms are on, if every reward dollar is matched by output, or if the price-damping rule is kept on. In the Stress Test the no-program row is the same as in the Adverse environment; the environments differ in how the program performs. A negative severity figure is good: it is the fall in poverty severity against no program (the range is 95%).

How to read the columns. Price rise is how much faster prices rise each year because of the program. Change in earnings is how much people's earnings change because they change how much they work in response to the program. Wealth poverty is the share of people with too little wealth. Below the cost of living is the share of person-years below the cost of living; severity weights the deepest shortfalls most.

Every gain should hold on all three measures. A design that lowers the first two while the third rises is not a confirmed gain. In the three rows above, poverty below the cost of living and below 30 days of basic living falls in every environment (except 30-days poverty in the Stress Test, where it rises), but too little wealth at year 20 rises in all three, so none of them is a confirmed gain on every measure. In the model that rise comes from prices rising while savings are not protected from inflation.

Part 3. The earlier engine (v4.22): six designs on the same simulated adults

Each design is paid for by the same flat contribution on wages, set each year to cover its cost, and adults adjust how much they work by the same rules under every design (earnings fall by 16 cents per unconditional dollar, the rate measured in the largest US study of unconditional cash). Poverty here is measured against a living-wage basket and the deepest shortfalls are weighted most (FGT2). Negative is less poverty. Brackets are 95% intervals across paired seeds. Today's safety net is not in these runs; Part 3 adds it.

Compassionism Core and Compassionism with its untested mechanisms (earlier engine)

The like-for-like row, Compassionism Core, is the engine's Compassionism with the two untested mechanisms switched off: the wage raise for each octave held, and slower inflation in proportion to membership of the Public Trust Foundations (community-owned businesses) and Public Trust Housing. It can be set beside the other designs without giving Compassionism extras the others do not get. The row above it in the table, Compassionism, as coded, has both mechanisms on, which is closer to the Hub's picture of the design but rests on two assumptions that are still theoretical; they carry most of Compassionism's edge in these runs. The button specified on the Hub switches to the Hub's written conversion design (businesses convert the Basic Units, the community currency, that they accept, and expired Basic Units go to projects that participants convert), which costs about two and a half to three times as much per adult. The model measures none of the cultural effects the framework holds make that cost worthwhile. Compassionism as designed differs from the Core in exactly these ways. Read the Core row for the like-for-like comparison and the others as the design's aim.

Limits

  • Part 3 (the earlier engine) predates the household, children, health and labour-market mechanisms and the safety net; it has not been re-run on the current engine. The like-for-like re-run of the six designs on the current engine, with the safety net in every arm, is Part 1.
  • The comparison designs are simplified versions of real proposals; each is described, with sources, on the earlier-engine page.
  • Public costs of poverty (crime, homelessness services, emergency health care) are credited in the current release but were not in Part 3 (the earlier engine), so no design there is credited with the savings from poverty it removes.
  • This page is kept out of search results until Duke approves it.
