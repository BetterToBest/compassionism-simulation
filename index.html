Compassionism aspires to enhance cultures by eradicating extreme poverty while incentivizing participation and contribution: see how it does.

The math and code of this simulation were engineered by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, from the concepts in Duke Johnson's book Better To Best and his related vision for eradicating extreme poverty while enriching cultures and supporting human flourishing. The model has not yet been reviewed by an independent economist; the full code is open for anyone to check, and expert collaborators are welcome.

An open agent-based model follows the same 500 simulated adults for 20 years, or for 40, once with Compassionism and once with no programme, in three environments. The figures below are averages over 500 paired runs; you can also run the model yourself.

What Compassionism is

  • Every adult who takes part receives a monthly allowance of Basic Units (BU): a currency that buys only essentials (food, housing, utilities, health care) and expires if it is not spent.
  • Expired BU can be converted to dollars at higher rates, for work the community values. They go to creative projects organized through the Creative Collectives (networks of artists, makers and other contributors). The projects hire participants, offer their work to members far below the market price, and are paid at the conversion rate they have earned by showing their work through the Collectives. A person's octave sets how much they can convert; it rises with their financial stability. Claude's reading of the design: what the projects deliver counts as new output at market value; the cautious reading, counting only the cost of their hours, is shown beside it.
  • Essential-service providers (ESPs), such as grocers, utilities and clinics, accept BU and convert them to dollars. Community-owned ESPs (Public Trust Foundations, PTFs) split what they earn between lower prices, new capacity and their workers. Privately owned ESPs pass what conversion adds to their BU customers as lower prices, and pay their workers what community-ESP workers earn: the market rate, since both compete for the same workers. Community ESPs add capacity within a year when demand outgrows them, paying for the capital they borrow.
  • Community-owned housing (Public Trust Housing, PTH) lowers housing costs and builds residents' equity; zone coordination (SZH) and a civic internet portal (CIP) complete the design.
  • Spending creates jobs in recessions: the programme's spending fills the idle capacity a recession leaves, at a multiplier from published estimates.
  • Any adult can join or leave each year.
  • How it is paid for: a Source (the Treasury) issues the BU and pays for every conversion, keeping the conversion tax. Claude's reading of the design: the Hub names no tax that pays the Source, so the model treats what it pays out as new money, except what new output backs.

Limits, in short. This is an exploratory model, not a forecast. Its results show what its assumptions imply; they do not show that the assumptions are true.
  • The decisive unknown is how much new output conversion rewards call forth. Treated as new money with little output behind it, they raise prices fast and erode savings; fully backed by output, they do not. The figures show both ends.
  • Single adults only: no children or households, and one country's prices and wages (US).
  • No places: community businesses and housing are national averages, and the design's local charters cannot be represented.
  • Prices respond to new money by a simple rule; there is no central bank, no interest on savings, and savings are not protected from inflation.
  • Public costs avoided count homelessness, prisons, hospital and psychiatric care, each from a published causal estimate; child welfare is not counted (the model has no children).
  • Spending creates jobs only in recessions: outside them the model has no idle capacity, so extra spending goes to prices.
  • The model has not been reviewed by an independent economist.
  • The full list of assumptions and limits, the calibration and the version history are on the replication page.

▶ 📖 Glossary new to this? tap to expand
CCO — Creative Currency Octaves

Dual-currency layer. Participants get monthly Basic Units (BU), spent on essentials at essential-service providers (ESPs), community-owned or private; expired BU are converted to primary currency (dollars) at merit-scaled rates through the Creative Collectives.

BU — Basic Units

The community currency issued by CCO. Pegged 1:1 to primary currency, accepted for essentials by essential-service providers (ESPs), community-owned (PTF) or private, and expires monthly.

ESP — Essential-service provider

A business that sells essentials (groceries, utilities, health care, meals) and accepts BU. Community-owned ESPs are PTFs; privately owned ESPs pass what conversion adds to their BU customers as lower prices.

Creative Collectives

Networks of artists, makers and other contributors, part of CCO, that review members' work and manage the conversion of expired BU into primary currency at elevated rates.

PTF — Public Trust Foundations

Community-owned, not-for-profit businesses that accept BU. Modelled as a cost-reduction mechanism (12–16% lower costs), not a wealth-addition channel.

PTH — Public Trust Housing

Community-owned housing; monthly payments build Acre Equity instead of paying market rent or mortgage interest.

AE — Acre Equity

Community equity built by PTH residents, appreciating ~4%/yr, accessible at exit subject to a tenure-based liquidity haircut.

SZH — Social Zone Harmonization

Spatial governance layer organizing where PTF/PTH deploy; gates cooperative synergy effects behind a zone-participation threshold.

CIP — Citizens Internet Portal

Digital platform administering CCO currency operations and democratic voting for PTH/PTF decisions.

BLEI — Basic Living Economic Index

Primary welfare metric: how many days of basic living an agent's accessible resources cover (cash + income + benefit buffers).

EDC — Extractive Drain Coefficient

Share of income captured by contracts that build zero household wealth (rent, interest, fees). PTH payments have EDC = 0.

FBS — Financial Bandwidth Score

Monthly residual resources above the basic-living threshold; gates an agent's probability of advancing an octave level.

Automatic Stabilizers (v4.19, opt-in)
Extra support that switches on by itself during a recession or inflation, modelled on the Research Hub's crisis protocols. Every lever is off by default. The recession levers act only when recessions are switched on, and the cost-of-living adjustment only when inflation is above its trigger (recessions and inflation are set under Economic Environment).
Raise BU during recessions ⓘ
In a recession year that meets the trigger below, each CCO participant's BU is raised for that year. Research Hub protocol: +20%.
How the raise is set
Fixed: the same multiplier in every triggered year. Scaled: a larger raise in a deeper recession.
BU multiplier (fixed rule)×1.35
×1.35 means BU is 35% higher in a triggered year. Research Hub: ×1.20. At the reference settings, ×1.35 fully offsets the extra housing distress a recession causes participants; the Shock Response card finds the value for your settings.
BU raise per 1% of income lost (scaled rule)+2.8%
At +2.8%, a recession that cuts income by 12% raises BU by about 34%. +2.8% is the scaled rule's equivalent of ×1.35 at the reference settings.
Trigger: smallest income loss that counts2%
The raise applies only in recession years that cut income by at least this much. Research Hub: output down more than 2%. Every recession in this model cuts income by 5–30%, so the 2% default fires in all of them; a higher trigger saves the raise for deeper recessions.
Let unspent BU carry over while triggered
Unspent BU normally expires on the BU-expiry schedule. In a triggered year it carries over instead, up to three allocations' worth. Research Hub disaster protocol: "suspend expiration".
Emergency enrollment of non-participants
People outside CCO receive CCO's cost relief in triggered years only. They do not earn octaves or convert BU. Research Hub: "relax requirements".
Emergency take-up (% of non-participants)50%
The share of non-participants who enroll when emergency enrollment opens. The 50% default is a placeholder: no source located (v4.19).
Cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) to BU ⓘ
Raises BU each year in step with prices, so its real value holds. Research Hub Inflation Surge Protocol.
Apply COLA when inflation is above0.0%
0% = always adjust. Research Hub: 5%. Inflation is one fixed rate for the whole run in this model, so COLA is either on for the entire run or not at all.

What each measure asks, and how it is counted

Why five measures. They answer different questions. The stock measures ask whether people have a cushion; the flow measures ask whether this year's income covers this year's needs; extreme poverty asks whether people are homeless. A policy can move one and not another, so each is reported against where people started (year 0) as well as against the Baseline.

Stock: what people own at the end of the run. Wealth poverty counts net wealth below POVERTY_LINE ($25,000). BLEI poverty counts people whose savings, income and BU cover fewer than 30 days of basic living costs. BLEI counts BU and cost relief from the day someone enrols, so its year-0 figure is shown under Baseline rules, with the figure under your settings' rules beneath it.

Flow: what people earn in the final year. Cash income is wage income plus CCO conversion proceeds; PTH appreciation is left out, as a capital gain. Relative income poverty counts cash income below 60% of that scenario's own median, so when most incomes rise together it can stay flat or even rise. Basket poverty compares income with the cost of a living-wage basket (LIVING_WAGE_ANNUAL, adjusted for inflation): "net" uses the prices each person actually pays after CCO, PTF and PTH cost relief; "gross" uses full prices. At year 0 the median person's wage income ($39,945) is below the basket ($49,370), so about two-thirds of people start in basket poverty. That starting gap is what drives the Baseline's decline (Known Limitations; CONTRIBUTING.md v4.17).

Extreme: homeless, with necessities from charity if at all. The engine models no housing, mental illness or vocation, and at the US rate (about 22 per 10,000 people, HUD's 2025 point-in-time count) this is about one person in a 500-agent run. So it is an estimated share built from three pathways, and these rows read per 10,000 people. Economic (73% at year 0) moves with the share of people in housing distress, whose income plus savings cannot cover this year's basket; CCO income and PTF and PTH cost relief lower it. Serious mental illness (25%) does not respond to income, so CCO alone leaves it unchanged; wellness zones, which need both PTH and SZH, lower it by up to half (Housing First trial evidence), scaled by zone coherence. Voluntary (2%) covers ascetics, itinerant monastics and renunciants; it is the same in every scenario, and the share will be updated as data sources become available. This definition differs from the World Bank's, which counts income below $3.00 a day. See Known Limitations and CONTRIBUTING.md v4.18.

Shock Response automatic BU increase in recessions, and what it offsets (v4.19)
How much of a recession's rise in housing distress an automatic BU increase offsets
How this is measured, and what it cannot show

Measure. Housing distress is the v4.18 measure: this year's cash income plus savings cannot cover this year's basket. For each seed the study runs your settings with recessions off and with recessions on under each rule, on the same agents, recession path and random draws. The excess is the difference in recession years, averaged over every recession year of every seed, for CCO participants and non-participants separately. Excess extreme poverty converts the all-agent excess through the v4.18 overlay's economic pathway. Extra BU is the added allocation as a share of the base allocation over the run.

Shock-neutral. The multiplier at which participants' excess distress in recession years is zero, found by false position from ×1 and ×3 (widened to ×4 if needed). At the reference settings it is ×1.36 over 300 seeds (node harness.js stabilizer), shown as ×1.35 on the slider; the scaled rule's equivalent is about +2.8% BU per 1% income loss. These are the sliders' defaults. The Research Hub's ×1.20 offsets about half of the rise. The neutral point is set by a roughly fixed amount of extra relief, about 7% of the basket, so it is the same in every environment at the same BU and 0% inflation, and higher where inflation erodes an unindexed BU: ×1.40 in Adverse Environment and ×1.55 in Stress Test (v4.21).

Why the default is a target, not the peak of a curve. Returns to a larger increase are close to linear, because the engine has no price response to BU issuance and no budget constraint. A larger increase never looks worse, and the model cannot test whether it would be inflationary. The default is the smallest increase that fully offsets a recession for the people it reaches.

How the BU amount reaches a household (v4.19). Through v4.18, CCO's cost relief was a flat 20% of the basket whatever the BU amount, so a larger BU helped only through conversion proceeds, which runYear() credits once a year. The framework describes BU as redeemable for essential goods, so from v4.19 the relief scales with the BU actually issued: 20% at the $1,200 reference, in proportion above and below it, up to a 50% ceiling (a placeholder). Every $1,200 preset is unchanged. Under the v4.18 rule the shock-neutral multiplier was ×2.35.

Reach. A BU increase reaches only CCO participants. Emergency enrollment extends the CCO cost relief to non-participants in triggered years; its 50% take-up default is a placeholder with no source.

Place-based BU premiums (not simulated)

The same lever can target places as well as times. A region that wants to attract residents, such as a cold-climate area with little winter sunlight, could add a BU premium for residents who meet a residency requirement, much as Alaska's Permanent Fund Dividend pays each qualifying resident after a full calendar year of residence. Whether such payments move people is not well established: a 2019 University of Alaska review lists the dividend's effect on in-migration among the questions not yet studied. The closest measured case, Tulsa Remote, pays remote workers $10,000 to relocate; an Upjohn Institute evaluation (Bartik, 2025) estimates that 58–70% of participants would not have moved without it. Remote workers are a different population from households in poverty, and the same evaluation notes that added housing supply is needed to realise the benefits. A payment tied to a place can also be absorbed by higher local rents; Public Trust Housing, whose costs are set outside the private rental market, could limit that. This simulation has no geography, so it reports no result for this use.

Rather than reporting poverty at one fixed dollar or day cutoff, these curves show a full range of thresholds at once — the "poverty dominance" approach used in the poverty-measurement literature (Atkinson 1987; the World Bank's own practice of reporting multiple poverty lines) to avoid resting a claim on any single contested figure. If a scenario's line sits below another's across the whole range shown, it reduces poverty no matter where the "true" line is drawn. "Avg. shortfall" (v4.11) answers a different question the headcount view can't: two scenarios can show identical % curves while differing a lot in how deep the remaining poverty runs — the poverty-gap view shows that depth directly, in the same dollars/days as the axis itself. See the Assumptions panel below for the full rationale, including why LIVING_WAGE_ANNUAL is a separate question from what's shown here. Reading the dominance check (v4.12, caveated v4.13): it is evaluated at the thresholds actually plotted, not continuously, so a crossing narrower than one grid step would not be reported; and it describes this single stochastic run, not a confidence statement — re-run with several seeds (or use Run 10×/50×) before treating a dominance result as a property of the configuration rather than of the draw.
