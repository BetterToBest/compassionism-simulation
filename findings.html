What the simulation finds

The math and code of this simulation were engineered by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, from the concepts in Duke Johnson's book Better To Best and his related vision for eradicating extreme poverty while enriching cultures and supporting human flourishing. The model has not yet been reviewed by an independent economist; the full code is open for anyone to check, and expert collaborators are welcome.

The headline results come first. Then comes a guided read of each, with a chart, and then every table behind them, with downloads. Pick a release, an environment and 20 or 40 years. The address carries your choice, so you can share any view.

Terms used on this page (glossary)
CCO — Creative Currency Octaves

Dual-currency layer. Participants get monthly Basic Units (BU), spent on essentials at essential-service providers (ESPs), community-owned or private; expired BU are converted to primary currency (dollars) at merit-scaled rates through the Creative Collectives.

BU — Basic Units

The community currency issued by CCO. Pegged 1:1 to primary currency, accepted for essentials by essential-service providers (ESPs), community-owned (PTF) or private, and expires monthly.

ESP — Essential-service provider

A business that sells essentials (groceries, utilities, health care, meals) and accepts BU. Community-owned ESPs are PTFs; privately owned ESPs pass what conversion adds to their BU customers as lower prices.

Creative Collectives

Networks of artists, makers and other contributors, part of CCO, that review members' work and manage the conversion of expired BU into primary currency at elevated rates.

PTF — Public Trust Foundations

Community-owned, not-for-profit businesses that accept BU. Modeled as a cost-reduction mechanism (12–16% lower costs), not a wealth-addition channel.

PTH — Public Trust Housing

Community-owned housing; monthly payments build Acre Equity instead of paying market rent or mortgage interest.

AE — Acre Equity

Community equity built by PTH residents, appreciating ~4%/yr, accessible at exit subject to a tenure-based liquidity haircut.

SZH — Social Zone Harmonization

Spatial governance layer organizing where PTF/PTH deploy; gates cooperative synergy effects behind a zone-participation threshold.

CIP — Citizens Internet Portal

Digital platform administering CCO currency operations and democratic voting for PTH/PTF decisions.

BLEI — Basic Living Economic Index

Primary welfare metric: how many days of basic living an agent's accessible resources cover (cash + income + benefit buffers). Two readings are shown: the BLEI paper's own, and the same-rules reading (earlier called design-neutral): the same rules for everyone: 12% of a month's wage and the ordinary daily cost of living, plus one month of whatever regular support a design pays; it leaves out the two extras the BLEI paper gives participants (20% of wage, and a lower daily cost in community housing).

EDC — Extractive Drain Coefficient

Share of income captured by contracts that build zero household wealth (rent, interest, fees). PTH payments have EDC = 0. The earlier engine's EDC figures are a design target proxy: a formula set to the BLEI paper's target bands, not measured from the model's flows (since v5.3 the model also measures it from rent and interest paid).

FBS — Financial Bandwidth Score

Monthly residual resources above the basic-living threshold; gates an agent's probability of advancing an octave level.

Reading the measures

How to read this measure. “Too little wealth” counts adults whose net wealth is below six months of their own cost of living: a reserve against a lost job or an emergency. Today that reserve is what stands between a setback and hardship, so people are right to build it. The framework expects people to need it less. When next month’s basics are guaranteed, and work becomes more a choice than a condition of security as AI and robotics take on more of it, many would stop saving a large reserve and spend more of their time and money on what they value: family, craft, learning, community, travel. In that world, smaller savings would not mean less security, and the gain would be measured in surveys of how people live and how they feel, more than in dollars (Duke Johnson’s framing; the Research Hub’s glossary, “Post-Scarcity Economics”). Economists see a version of this today: a guaranteed minimum reduces how much lower-income households save for emergencies (Hubbard, Skinner and Zeldes, Journal of Political Economy, 1995). What the model does not do: its adults spend the same share of income above their costs with and without the program (the share that matches today’s US saving rate), so no one here saves less because they feel secure, and the model measures no well-being by survey. The gap above comes from the model’s mechanics (prices rising while savings earn nothing, and lower pay after a job loss), not from that change of mindset; the framing explains how the measure should be read in a guaranteed-minimum world, and does not turn the result into a gain.
