What the simulation finds

The math and code of this simulation were engineered by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, from the concepts in Duke Johnson's book Better To Best and his related vision for eradicating extreme poverty while enriching cultures and supporting human flourishing. The model has not yet been reviewed by an independent economist; the full code is open for anyone to check, and expert collaborators are welcome.

The newest model comes first: v5.9, with a switch for the central bank. v5.6, the earlier panel, follows for comparison, then the preregistered studies and every table, with downloads. Pick a release, an environment and 20 or 40 years; the address keeps your choice, so any view can be shared.

Terms used on this page (glossary)
OEIAS — Optional Equitable Integrated Assurance Systems

The umbrella name for the five architectures taken together (CCO, PTF, PTH, SZH and CIP), with X-Cents. It supplements Compassionism and the label CCO-PTF-PTH-CIP-SZH; it replaces neither, and adds no new mechanism or result. The Normative Economic Evaluation (NEEC) uses CCO-PTF-PTH-CIP-SZH for the earlier version of this simulation and OEIAS for the latest.

CCO — Creative Currency Octaves

Dual-currency layer. Participants get monthly Basic Units (BU), spent on essentials at essential-service providers (ESPs), community-owned or private; expired BU are converted to primary currency (dollars) at merit-scaled rates through the Creative Collectives.

BU — Basic Units

The community currency issued by CCO. Pegged 1:1 to primary currency, accepted for essentials by essential-service providers (ESPs), community-owned (PTF) or private, and expires monthly.

ESP — Essential-service provider

A business that sells essentials (groceries, utilities, health care, meals) and accepts BU. Community-owned ESPs are PTFs; privately owned ESPs pass what conversion adds to their BU customers as lower prices.

Creative Collectives

Networks of artists, makers and other contributors, part of CCO, that review members' work and manage the conversion of expired BU into primary currency at elevated rates.

PTF — Public Trust Foundations

Community-owned, not-for-profit businesses that accept BU. Modeled as a cost-reduction mechanism (12–16% lower costs), not a wealth-addition channel.

PTH — Public Trust Housing

Community-owned housing; monthly payments build Acre Equity instead of paying market rent or mortgage interest.

AE — Acre Equity

Community equity built by PTH residents, appreciating ~4%/yr, accessible at exit subject to a tenure-based liquidity haircut.

SZH — Social Zone Harmonization

Spatial governance layer organizing where PTF/PTH deploy; gates cooperative synergy effects behind a zone-participation threshold.

CIP — Citizens Internet Portal

Digital platform administering CCO currency operations and democratic voting for PTH/PTF decisions.

BLEI — Basic Living Economic Index

Primary welfare metric: how many days of basic living an agent's accessible resources cover (cash + income + benefit buffers). Two readings are shown: the BLEI paper's own, and the same-rules reading (earlier called design-neutral): the same rules for everyone: 12% of a month's wage and the ordinary daily cost of living, plus one month of whatever regular support a design pays; it leaves out the two extras the BLEI paper gives participants (20% of wage, and a lower daily cost in community housing).

EDC — Extractive Drain Coefficient

Share of income captured by contracts that build zero household wealth (rent, interest, fees). PTH payments have EDC = 0. The earlier engine's EDC figures are a design target proxy: a formula set to the BLEI paper's target bands, not measured from the model's flows (since v5.3 the model also measures it from rent and interest paid).

FBS — Financial Bandwidth Score

Monthly residual resources above the basic-living threshold; gates an agent's probability of advancing an octave level.

Basket poverty

The share of people (children counted in their households) whose household income falls short of that household’s cost of a basic living, the MIT living-wage basket for its adults and children. Shown as a share of person-years.

Wealth poverty

The share of adults at the end of the run whose net wealth (what they own less what they owe; in a couple, half of what the two own) is below the threshold of $25,000 in today’s money. The threshold is a design threshold chosen for this page, not a measured line. It moves with inflation: with prices rising and savings earning nothing, more adults fall below it.

Programme inflation

The yearly rise in the price level that the programme causes, as the model computes it. It rests on the model’s placeholder price rule: all new money the programme pays that is not matched by new output reaches prices. It is a result of that rule, not a forecast.

Backing and financing options

Backing is how much of the money the Source pays out is matched by new output. The headline reading is cautious: none is. “If every dollar were backed” is a stated alternative. Financing options pay the programme by taxes instead of new money: a flat contribution on wages, or a land-value tax.

Participation

The share of adults who take part in the programme. In the opt-in design adults choose to take part; in the full-coverage design everyone is covered automatically, so a participation figure applies only to the first.

Inflation-protected savings

Savings whose value keeps pace with prices: interest equals inflation (zero real return). It is a reading that removes the programme’s erosion of savings; it is not a test of a positive return. The page shows it beside ordinary savings, which earn nothing in real terms.

The three environments

Reference: the model’s main settings, with no recessions and no outside inflation. Adverse: recessions, outside inflation and an automation wave. Stress Test: the Adverse environment with weaker settings (fewer take part, a smaller allowance). The figures for each are in the release file.

Percentage points

A change in a share is given in percentage points (pts): the difference between two percentages, each measured against the same base, which is named next to the figure (usually the no-programme run of the same seeds). A fall from one share to a lower one is stated in points, never as a percentage of the first.

The central bank switch #

v5.9 has no central bank in its main row. The central-bank study adds one as a switch: a policy rate set by a Taylor rule (floored at zero, with bond purchases below the floor), which moves deposit and loan rates, refinancing, share and home prices and job loss, and lets households past their borrowing limit sell homes and shares to households with cash. It asks two questions: does the central bank steady households, and does the programme’s share of essentials damp what the central bank and outside shocks do to the cost of basic living? The rule is the same with and without the programme. History follows the actual US path of 2005-2025.

The central-bank study loads with its data file.

How the Federal Reserve is governed, and why the rule is not the Fed

The Board of Governors is appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Each of the twelve Reserve Banks has nine directors, six of them elected by the member banks of its district; since the Dodd-Frank Act (2010), the directors who represent banks no longer vote on choosing the Reserve Bank’s president. The model does not represent these choices: its rule is a fixed formula of inflation and unemployment, the same in every run, so the comparison is between rules, not institutions.

Duke Johnson’s note on the design’s aim: central-bank decisions made by an unelected board, with private banks’ influence over it, shape millions of lives; quantitative easing supported Wall Street more than homeowners; official inflation is an average that misses what many households face; and citizens collectively ensuring essential markets would remove the volatility that wealthy interests can exploit by buying low and selling high. Compassionism’s stated aim is stability and prosperity rather than exploitation and extraction. The model tests the parts it can measure (who gains and who pays, forced sales, the stability measures above).

Earlier eras: the Research Hub’s page US Policy Eras and Stability, 1947-2025 compares the postwar eras’ inflation, unemployment, prices, tax rates and health care with 2005-2025, and runs today’s households under each era’s settings. Its engine readings were run without the release’s global settings (the same omission found in the central-bank study) and are being rerun; its descriptive panel is not affected.

Reading the measures

How to read this measure. “Too little wealth” counts adults whose net wealth is below six months of their own cost of living: a reserve against a lost job or an emergency. Today that reserve is what stands between a setback and hardship, so people are right to build it. The framework expects people to need it less. When next month’s basics are guaranteed, and work becomes more a choice than a condition of security as AI and robotics take on more of it, many would stop saving a large reserve and spend more of their time and money on what they value: family, craft, learning, community, travel. In that world, smaller savings would not mean less security, and the gain would be measured in surveys of how people live and how they feel, more than in dollars (Duke Johnson’s framing; the Research Hub’s glossary, “Post-Scarcity Economics”). Economists see a version of this today: a guaranteed minimum reduces how much lower-income households save for emergencies (Hubbard, Skinner and Zeldes, Journal of Political Economy, 1995). What the model does not do: its adults spend the same share of income above their costs with and without the program (the share that matches today’s US saving rate), so no one here saves less because they feel secure, and the model measures no well-being by survey. The gap above comes from the model’s mechanics (prices rising while savings earn nothing, and lower pay after a job loss), not from that change of mindset; the framing explains how the measure should be read in a guaranteed-minimum world, and does not turn the result into a gain.
