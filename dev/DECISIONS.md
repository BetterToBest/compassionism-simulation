## Step 1: the ESP split (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

The design and its first ten decisions (d97 to d106) are in `dev/background/N1-design-esp-surplus.md`; those defaults stand except where an entry below changes one.

- **How the model tells a community business (PTF) from a private essential-service business.** Chosen: the PTFs' share of the ESP premium each year is the share of last year's BU that PTF members spent (PTF members are read as the PTFs' customers). Alternatives: one split for every ESP (the earlier d102 default); the PTF share of essentials spending, cash included. Why: the premium comes only from converting BU, so the BU PTF members spent is the closest thing the model has to "BU accepted by PTFs". It needs no new constant.
- **Private ESPs.** Chosen, following Duke's Oct 1 answer (owners keep all of a private business's earnings): the private share of the premium is paid in dollars to owners, every adult in proportion to their positive wealth at the start of the year. It counts as one unconditional dollar (one income effect), like any dividend. Alternatives: by wage; to the top third only. Why: the model has no firms or shares; wealth is the standard stand-in for who owns businesses, and proportional-to-wealth needs no new constant. This makes the split the main row's default (the earlier d102 default, one split for all, is kept as a sensitivity row).
- **Who gets the PTFs' price cuts.** Chosen: PTF members, the same customers the PTF share is measured on. Alternatives: every adult (d98's default, kept as a row); participants and PTF members; participants. Why: consistency. If PTF members are the PTFs' customers when sizing the PTFs' share, the PTFs' lower prices reach the same people.
- **Who gets the PTFs' profit share.** Chosen: every ESP worker (d72's 23.0% of adults), by last year's wage, as in d104. Alternative: only ESP workers who are PTF members. Why: the model does not link a worker to a particular business; spreading the PTF profit share over all ESP workers keeps d104's rule and changes only the size of each share.
- **Capacity trigger under the owners rule.** Chosen: unchanged K ($18,862 per adult), reached only by the PTFs' reinvestment. Alternative: scale K by the PTFs' share. Why: the Hub's phase 2 starts when there are enough PTFs to serve demand; the capital needed for that does not shrink because PTFs earn less. Consequence reported: phase 2 comes much later than in the sizing.
- **The design-neutral BLEI and the price cut.** Chosen: the neutral BLEI gate keeps counting the BU a participant would have spent without the cut. Alternative: count only the BU spent after the cut. Why: no price cut enters any design's neutral BLEI (d34), so a cut that replaces BU with a lower price should not look like lost support.
- **Reinvestment spent as price cuts (the d100 sensitivity).** Chosen: no capital is built, so phase 1 lasts the whole run. Why: money spent on prices does not build capacity.
- **Exact equivalence tests.** The profit share and "rest paid as today" paths are written to reproduce today's payout and ESP payroll's worker-cooperative row to the last digit (unit tests), so any difference in the restudy is the split itself.

## Step 2: the production side (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **What the capital the ESP split reinvests does.** Chosen: it is PTF capacity. The PTFs can serve a share of adults equal to the PTF members they had at the start plus the capital account per adult divided by $18,862 (the 2025 capital stock per adult of grocers, food services and utilities). New PTF members join only while there is room; nobody is removed. Alternatives: let capital add essentials supply in the price module (kept as a sensitivity row, "capacity term at 1"); keep PTF reach unlimited (today). Why: until now the model gave PTFs unlimited capacity from year 0 at no cost, so building capacity showed its cost and never its benefit. Linking reach to capital uses the same sourced figure as the phase-2 trigger and needs no new constant. In the model's price rule, food prices do not respond to supply (food has an outside market), so more grocers lower prices mainly by bringing PTF prices to more people.
- **When phase 2 starts with capacity on.** Chosen: when PTF capacity reaches every adult (existing PTFs plus reinvestment), which comes a little earlier than reinvestment alone reaching $18,862. Why: the Hub's rule is "enough PTFs per town to serve demand", and the existing PTFs count.
- **Project work as output.** Chosen: valued at the contract pay for the hours (the BU's face value). The conversion reward above that is not output. Alternative: the workers' own market wage (a sensitivity row). Why: design default 1 counts project work "at the cost of the hours and materials it pays for"; the contract pay is that cost, and it credits less output than most workers' own wages, so it is the cautious choice. The model has no materials, so none are counted (a stated limit).
- **Reinvestment as output.** Chosen: counted at face value (capital goods are produced). Neither kind adds essentials supply (design default 1).
- **Where matched output acts.** Chosen: only where conversion is new money (money, hybrid and, from step 3, Source financing): new money = (1 − a) × (conversion − matched output). H1 (a = 1) stays a sensitivity row. Under the wage contribution it changes nothing, which a unit test checks to the last digit.

## Step 3: how Compassionism pays for itself (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

My reading of the Hub is in `dev/reports/03a-financing-reading.md`.

- **Who pays the BU.** Chosen: a Source (the Treasury) issues the BU, pays every conversion (the face value of BU that businesses accept, and every conversion reward), and keeps the conversion tax; expired BU return to it and go to projects. Alternative: the flat wage contribution (kept as a sensitivity row). Why: the Hub's model legislation calls BU a currency "created under this Act" and paid through the Treasury, and describes no wage tax for them.
- **Where the Source's dollars come from.** Chosen: its net payout is new money, less the output that backs it (step 2), with H1 (all of it backed) as a sensitivity. Alternative: a tax the Hub does not name. Why: the Hub names no other source ("full creative reserve"; conversion as the channel of monetary expansion). This is the cautious reading, because it lets the model show the inflation the design could cause instead of assuming it away.
- **The face value of essentials bought with BU.** Chosen: counted as new money. Alternative (a sensitivity row): counted as backed by the essentials produced and sold. Why: the essentials are part of what people already consume; BU paying for them frees other money, so the cautious reading treats it as new money.
- **What the contribution still pays.** Chosen: only what the Source does not: the PTF and PTH price cuts and PTH appreciation, until step 5 costs them. Why: these are not conversions.

## Step 4: joining and leaving (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **What "what they would pay" means.** In this model a participant pays nothing a non-participant does not (the wage contribution falls on every adult), so a literal reading makes every adult join. Chosen: the cost of taking part (time, obligations) is revealed by the year-0 choice: adult i's yearly cost is 12 × BU × (their latent participation draw ÷ the participation rate), in year-0 dollars, moved with prices. At year 0 this reproduces the model's participation exactly; later, an adult joins or leaves when the BU they would use (up to their essentials at their own prices) rise above or fall below it. Alternatives (sensitivity rows): no cost (every adult joins, the literal reading); the cost fixed in dollars. Why: it keeps the participation rates the environments were built on, adds no unsourced constant, and lets the rule respond only to what the design changes: the value of the BU.
- **Minimum stay** 2 years (swept 1 and 5); rejoining allowed; no fee. Emergency enrolment unchanged.
- **Groups.** "Participants" and "non-participants" in every table stay the adults who took part at year 0, so the same people are compared across rows.

## Step 5: PTF running costs and PTH capital (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **PTF running costs.** Chosen: PTF capital comes from the reinvestment account (steps 1-2), so a PTF need not earn a return on capital and can price at running cost: the conventional price less the industry's net operating surplus (BEA 2025, operating surplus less depreciation; grocers' margin converted to sales with Census's 28.8% gross margin). That funds 4.94% off food and 2.60% off the housing component (utilities), about 1.2% of the basket, roughly 8% of the model's PTF member discount. That part is not a programme cost; the rest of the discount is a running subsidy, counted as before. Alternatives: cut the PTF discount to what forgone profit funds (changes the design's 12%; not taken); "price cuts free" (a sensitivity row). Why: it keeps your discount and prices it from sourced figures.
- **PTH capital.** Chosen: costed and reported, not added: $156,000 of structures per rented home (BEA tenant-occupied residential stock / Census renter households, 2025). The housing industry's net operating surplus is 54.7% of rents (BEA 2025), so PTH's 35% cut is the return the PTH forgoes on that capital; its cost is the cut, which the model already counts. Alternative: count the capital outlay as well (would count the same cost twice). Why: residents' reduced rent still covers running costs, taxes and depreciation, as the Hub's pay-in model says ("monthly housing costs reduced to taxes, insurance, maintenance").

## Steps 6-9 (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **Octave rule.** Kept as it is (advancement more likely the more financial breathing room an adult has), described in plain words on the page. Slower advancement (one octave per 2, 3 or 5 years) is a sensitivity. Why: design default 4.
- **Spending rule.** Chosen: adults spend 59.3% of cash income above the cost of living, the share that reproduces the 2025 US personal saving rate (5.4%, BEA Table 2.1) on the no-programme run; it applies to every row and the Baseline from step 7 on. Alternatives: the old placeholder 90%; the page's 0%; 2024's rate (55.4%) as a row. Why: it is the only one of these tied to a measured figure. It is derived once on the no-programme run, not tuned to a programme result. The derivation uses the model's own saving measure (wages less the basket actually consumed and the surplus spent, over wages).
- **BLEI-led reporting.** Every step's output now opens with BLEI: your BLEI paper's definition first, the design-neutral reading beside it, both with intervals. Design default 8 stands; the step-8 report shows the two readings side by side so the choice can be seen.
- **Avoided public costs.** Homelessness only, with the health and justice costs that come with it, costed at two sourced ends ($10,462 and $100,608 per homeless person-year, 2025 dollars). Health care and crime among poor adults who are housed are not costed: I found no primary source giving a causal public cost per poor adult-year, so the report says the figure understates avoided costs rather than inventing one.

## Session 31 (Oct 2, 2026): the four items Duke added before v5.0

- **Oct 2, 2026 (session 31). Private ESPs (correction d140).** The conversion premium at a privately owned ESP becomes a lower BU price for the customer (revenue neutral); no premium goes to owners. Participating private-ESP workers get the community-ESP profit share; a second row caps that at the sourced margin on added sales. Alternatives: keep the owners' branch (wrong per Duke); fund the pay match without a check (hides an assumption). Why: Duke's own description; the cap row keeps the funding assumption visible. `dev/reports/v5-1-private-esp-correction.md`.
- **Oct 2, 2026 (session 31). Creative-output backing (d138).** Main run: the elevated part of a creative payout counts as output at market value, but only for delivered (shown) work; a group with nothing shown converts at 1x. Other reading: cost of hours and materials (session 30 rule). Sensitivity: every dollar backed (H1). Alternatives: the cost rule alone (ignores Duke's answer); market value without the shown-work condition (counts output that may not exist). Why: Duke's revenue-neutral design is honoured, while the cautious reading stays on the page because output that would have existed anyway backs nothing.
- **Oct 2, 2026 (session 31). Expired BU to Creative Collectives.** A share of expired BU may pay Collective groups; the share is not stated in Duke's writing, so it is a labelled design parameter swept at 0, 25% and 50%. Why: no source exists for it.
- **Oct 2, 2026 (session 31). Octave limit (d139).** ESPs' conversion is capped by their capacity; individuals and Collectives have no cap. The economy-wide brake therefore comes from ESP capacity. Alternative: a cap on total conversion across everyone (not Duke's design).
- **Oct 2, 2026 (session 31). Capacity speed (d137).** Main: capacity meets the previous year's unmet BU demand within a year, with its capital charged yearly at depreciation plus interest (BEA Fixed Assets, Fed H.15); other reading: the 5-year reinvestment rule. Alternative: within a year at no capital cost (makes fast capacity free, which flatters the result).
- **Oct 2, 2026 (session 31). Spending layer multiplier (s68).** 1.5 in recession years and 0.5 in normal years (main); 0.8 and 0 (low); 2.2 and 0.8 (high). Sources: CBO (Recovery Act transfers 0.8-2.2), Auerbach-Gorodnichenko 2012, Ramey-Zubairy 2018. Added output capped at idle capacity; re-employment earnings go first to adults who lost work, deterministically. Alternative: one multiplier for all years (the evidence says it depends on slack).
- **Oct 2, 2026 (session 31). Wider avoided costs (s69).** Counted only with a causal estimate of income's effect; zero main where none exists, the descriptive gap shown as an upper bound. Policing and courts zero main (mostly fixed costs); child welfare not costed until the model has children. Alternative: count the whole poor/non-poor gap (overstates; poor people differ in many ways).

## Session 31 (Oct 2, 2026): decisions made while building steps 14-18

- **The private-ESP workers' match is paid from the premium itself, not from "extra sales".** The model's basket is fixed, so lower BU prices free BU instead of adding sales; there is nothing else to fund the match. A private ESP gives its workers the community ESPs' profit-share fraction of its premium and passes the rest to BU customers. Alternatives: fund the match outside the premium (unaccounted new money); cap it at a margin on added sales (zero in this model). Row kept: the whole premium to prices.
- **Who gets a private ESP's lower prices.** Participants who are not community-business members, in proportion to the BU each spent last year, capped at their own essentials. Why: the model already reads community-business members as the community businesses' customers, so everyone else's BU were spent at private ones; weighting by BU spent is Duke's "1 BU buys r dollars" in model terms.
- **No new switch for the ESP octave cap, uncapped individuals or expired BU to Collectives.** The model already satisfies all three (BU only buy essentials businesses already supply; project hiring is uncapped; every expired BU pays project work). A unit test checks the first. The 0/25/50% sweep in the design note is dropped because the model's existing rule (all expired BU) is Duke's description.
- **Capacity within a year: the borrowed capital's charge** is 8.20% a year (BEA depreciation 4.53%; real Baa rate 3.67% from FRED 2025 means), on capital in year-0 dollars revalued with prices, paid from the community businesses' premium before the split; unpaid charges carry. The interest goes to lenders the model does not have (counted as new money, a stated limit). Alternative: a nominal rate (borrowing would cost almost nothing in real terms at the model's inflation).
- **The spending layer works only in recessions.** The model has no slack outside recessions (no involuntary unemployment), so the published normal-times multipliers (0-0.5) never apply; automation's wage losses are structural and not filled. The push is last year's net Source payout; the wages restored are min(labour share 0.508 x multiplier x push, the recession's wage loss). Alternative: apply multipliers in every year (would treat a 30%-of-income programme as if the economy always had room for it).
- **Wider avoided costs: prisons scaled by incarceration rates.** The prison effect comes from youth losing disability income, whose incarceration rate (4.7% a year) is far above US adults' (0.70%), so the main figure scales it by 0.70/4.7; the study's own figure is the high end; police and courts only in the high end (mostly fixed costs). Hospital and psychiatric savings use Forget's 8.5% on person-years lifted above the cost of living; emergency rooms only in the high end.
- **v5.0's main row** = session 30's release + steps 14-16. The readings shown beside it: H1, essentials backed, creative work at cost, the 5-year capacity rule, wage contribution, everyone joins, price cuts free, the former stand-ins, session 30's build, and v4.22. The "one split for every ESP" row is dropped: with the correction no private owner keeps the premium, so the row no longer describes an alternative.
