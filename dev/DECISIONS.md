## Step 1: the ESP split (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

The design and its first ten decisions (d97 to d106) are in `dev/background/N1-design-esp-surplus.md`; those defaults stand except where an entry below changes one.

- **How the model tells a community business (PTF) from a private essential-service business.** Chosen: the PTFs' share of the ESP premium each year is the share of last year's BU that PTF members spent (PTF members are read as the PTFs' customers). Alternatives: one split for every ESP (the earlier d102 default); the PTF share of essentials spending, cash included. Why: the premium comes only from converting BU, so the BU PTF members spent is the closest thing the model has to "BU accepted by PTFs". It needs no new constant.
- **Private ESPs.** Chosen, following Duke's Oct 1 answer (owners keep all of a private business's earnings): the private share of the premium is paid in dollars to owners, every adult in proportion to their positive wealth at the start of the year. It counts as one unconditional dollar (one income effect), like any dividend. Alternatives: by wage; to the top third only. Why: the model has no firms or shares; wealth is the standard stand-in for who owns businesses, and proportional-to-wealth needs no new constant. This makes the split the main row's default (the earlier d102 default, one split for all, is kept as a sensitivity row).
- **Who gets the PTFs' price cuts.** Chosen: PTF members, the same customers the PTF share is measured on. Alternatives: every adult (d98's default, kept as a row); participants and PTF members; participants. Why: consistency. If PTF members are the PTFs' customers when sizing the PTFs' share, the PTFs' lower prices reach the same people.
- **Who gets the PTFs' profit share.** Chosen: every ESP worker (d72's 23.0% of adults), by last year's wage, as in d104. Alternative: only ESP workers who are PTF members. Why: the model does not link a worker to a particular business; spreading the PTF profit share over all ESP workers keeps d104's rule and changes only the size of each share.
- **Capacity trigger under the owners rule.** Chosen: unchanged K ($18,862 per adult), reached only by the PTFs' reinvestment. Alternative: scale K by the PTFs' share. Why: the Hub's phase 2 starts when there are enough PTFs to serve demand; the capital needed for that does not shrink because PTFs earn less. Consequence reported: phase 2 comes much later than in the sizing.
- **The design-neutral BLEI and the price cut.** Chosen: the neutral BLEI gate keeps counting the BU a participant would have spent without the cut. Alternative: count only the BU spent after the cut. Why: no price cut enters any design's neutral BLEI (d34), so a cut that replaces BU with a lower price should not look like lost support.
- **Reinvestment spent as price cuts (the d100 sensitivity).** Chosen: no capital is built, so phase 1 lasts the whole run. Why: money spent on prices does not build capacity.
- **Exact equivalence tests.** The profit share and "rest paid as today" paths are written to reproduce today's payout and ESP payroll's worker-cooperative row to the last digit (unit tests), so any difference in the restudy is the split itself.

## Step 2: the production side (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **What the capital the ESP split reinvests does.** Chosen: it is PTF capacity. The PTFs can serve a share of adults equal to the PTF members they had at the start plus the capital account per adult divided by $18,862 (the 2025 capital stock per adult of grocers, food services and utilities). New PTF members join only while there is room; nobody is removed. Alternatives: let capital add essentials supply in the price module (kept as a sensitivity row, "capacity term at 1"); keep PTF reach unlimited (today). Why: until now the model gave PTFs unlimited capacity from year 0 at no cost, so building capacity showed its cost and never its benefit. Linking reach to capital uses the same sourced figure as the phase-2 trigger and needs no new constant. In the model's price rule, food prices do not respond to supply (food has an outside market), so more grocers lower prices mainly by bringing PTF prices to more people.
- **When phase 2 starts with capacity on.** Chosen: when PTF capacity reaches every adult (existing PTFs plus reinvestment), which comes a little earlier than reinvestment alone reaching $18,862. Why: the Hub's rule is "enough PTFs per town to serve demand", and the existing PTFs count.
- **Project work as output.** Chosen: valued at the contract pay for the hours (the BU's face value). The conversion reward above that is not output. Alternative: the workers' own market wage (a sensitivity row). Why: design default 1 counts project work "at the cost of the hours and materials it pays for"; the contract pay is that cost, and it credits less output than most workers' own wages, so it is the cautious choice. The model has no materials, so none are counted (a stated limit).
- **Reinvestment as output.** Chosen: counted at face value (capital goods are produced). Neither kind adds essentials supply (design default 1).
- **Where matched output acts.** Chosen: only where conversion is new money (money, hybrid and, from step 3, Source financing): new money = (1 − a) × (conversion − matched output). H1 (a = 1) stays a sensitivity row. Under the wage contribution it changes nothing, which a unit test checks to the last digit.

## Step 3: how Compassionism pays for itself (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

My reading of the Hub is in `dev/reports/03a-financing-reading.md`.

- **Who pays the BU.** Chosen: a Source (the Treasury) issues the BU, pays every conversion (the face value of BU that businesses accept, and every conversion reward), and keeps the conversion tax; expired BU return to it and go to projects. Alternative: the flat wage contribution (kept as a sensitivity row). Why: the Hub's model legislation calls BU a currency "created under this Act" and paid through the Treasury, and describes no wage tax for them.
- **Where the Source's dollars come from.** Chosen: its net payout is new money, less the output that backs it (step 2), with H1 (all of it backed) as a sensitivity. Alternative: a tax the Hub does not name. Why: the Hub names no other source ("full creative reserve"; conversion as the channel of monetary expansion). This is the cautious reading, because it lets the model show the inflation the design could cause instead of assuming it away.
- **The face value of essentials bought with BU.** Chosen: counted as new money. Alternative (a sensitivity row): counted as backed by the essentials produced and sold. Why: the essentials are part of what people already consume; BU paying for them frees other money, so the cautious reading treats it as new money.
- **What the contribution still pays.** Chosen: only what the Source does not: the PTF and PTH price cuts and PTH appreciation, until step 5 costs them. Why: these are not conversions.

## Step 4: joining and leaving (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **What "what they would pay" means.** In this model a participant pays nothing a non-participant does not (the wage contribution falls on every adult), so a literal reading makes every adult join. Chosen: the cost of taking part (time, obligations) is revealed by the year-0 choice: adult i's yearly cost is 12 × BU × (their latent participation draw ÷ the participation rate), in year-0 dollars, moved with prices. At year 0 this reproduces the model's participation exactly; later, an adult joins or leaves when the BU they would use (up to their essentials at their own prices) rise above or fall below it. Alternatives (sensitivity rows): no cost (every adult joins, the literal reading); the cost fixed in dollars. Why: it keeps the participation rates the environments were built on, adds no unsourced constant, and lets the rule respond only to what the design changes: the value of the BU.
- **Minimum stay** 2 years (swept 1 and 5); rejoining allowed; no fee. Emergency enrolment unchanged.
- **Groups.** "Participants" and "non-participants" in every table stay the adults who took part at year 0, so the same people are compared across rows.

## Step 5: PTF running costs and PTH capital (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **PTF running costs.** Chosen: PTF capital comes from the reinvestment account (steps 1-2), so a PTF need not earn a return on capital and can price at running cost: the conventional price less the industry's net operating surplus (BEA 2025, operating surplus less depreciation; grocers' margin converted to sales with Census's 28.8% gross margin). That funds 4.94% off food and 2.60% off the housing component (utilities), about 1.2% of the basket, roughly 8% of the model's PTF member discount. That part is not a programme cost; the rest of the discount is a running subsidy, counted as before. Alternatives: cut the PTF discount to what forgone profit funds (changes the design's 12%; not taken); "price cuts free" (a sensitivity row). Why: it keeps your discount and prices it from sourced figures.
- **PTH capital.** Chosen: costed and reported, not added: $156,000 of structures per rented home (BEA tenant-occupied residential stock / Census renter households, 2025). The housing industry's net operating surplus is 54.7% of rents (BEA 2025), so PTH's 35% cut is the return the PTH forgoes on that capital; its cost is the cut, which the model already counts. Alternative: count the capital outlay as well (would count the same cost twice). Why: residents' reduced rent still covers running costs, taxes and depreciation, as the Hub's pay-in model says ("monthly housing costs reduced to taxes, insurance, maintenance").

## Steps 6-9 (Oct 1, 2026, session 30)

- **Octave rule.** Kept as it is (advancement more likely the more financial breathing room an adult has), described in plain words on the page. Slower advancement (one octave per 2, 3 or 5 years) is a sensitivity. Why: design default 4.
- **Spending rule.** Chosen: adults spend 59.3% of cash income above the cost of living, the share that reproduces the 2025 US personal saving rate (5.4%, BEA Table 2.1) on the no-programme run; it applies to every row and the Baseline from step 7 on. Alternatives: the old placeholder 90%; the page's 0%; 2024's rate (55.4%) as a row. Why: it is the only one of these tied to a measured figure. It is derived once on the no-programme run, not tuned to a programme result. The derivation uses the model's own saving measure (wages less the basket actually consumed and the surplus spent, over wages).
- **BLEI-led reporting.** Every step's output now opens with BLEI: your BLEI paper's definition first, the design-neutral reading beside it, both with intervals. Design default 8 stands; the step-8 report shows the two readings side by side so the choice can be seen.
- **Avoided public costs.** Homelessness only, with the health and justice costs that come with it, costed at two sourced ends ($10,462 and $100,608 per homeless person-year, 2025 dollars). Health care and crime among poor adults who are housed are not costed: I found no primary source giving a causal public cost per poor adult-year, so the report says the figure understates avoided costs rather than inventing one.

## Session 31 (Oct 2, 2026): decisions made while building steps 14-18

- **The private-ESP workers' match is paid from the premium itself, not from "extra sales".** The model's basket is fixed, so lower BU prices free BU instead of adding sales; there is nothing else to fund the match. A private ESP gives its workers the community ESPs' profit-share fraction of its premium and passes the rest to BU customers. Alternatives: fund the match outside the premium (unaccounted new money); cap it at a margin on added sales (zero in this model). Row kept: the whole premium to prices.
- **Who gets a private ESP's lower prices.** Participants who are not community-business members, in proportion to the BU each spent last year, capped at their own essentials. Why: the model already reads community-business members as the community businesses' customers, so everyone else's BU were spent at private ones; weighting by BU spent is Duke's "1 BU buys r dollars" in model terms.
- **No new switch for the ESP octave cap, uncapped individuals or expired BU to Collectives.** The model already satisfies all three (BU only buy essentials businesses already supply; project hiring is uncapped; every expired BU pays project work). A unit test checks the first. The 0/25/50% sweep in the design note is dropped because the model's existing rule (all expired BU) is Duke's description.
- **Capacity within a year: the borrowed capital's charge** is 8.20% a year (BEA depreciation 4.53%; real Baa rate 3.67% from FRED 2025 means), on capital in year-0 dollars revalued with prices, paid from the community businesses' premium before the split; unpaid charges carry. The interest goes to lenders the model does not have (counted as new money, a stated limit). Alternative: a nominal rate (borrowing would cost almost nothing in real terms at the model's inflation).
- **The spending layer works only in recessions.** The model has no slack outside recessions (no involuntary unemployment), so the published normal-times multipliers (0-0.5) never apply; automation's wage losses are structural and not filled. The push is last year's net Source payout; the wages restored are min(labour share 0.508 x multiplier x push, the recession's wage loss). Alternative: apply multipliers in every year (would treat a 30%-of-income programme as if the economy always had room for it).
- **Wider avoided costs: prisons scaled by incarceration rates.** The prison effect comes from youth losing disability income, whose incarceration rate (4.7% a year) is far above US adults' (0.70%), so the main figure scales it by 0.70/4.7; the study's own figure is the high end; police and courts only in the high end (mostly fixed costs). Hospital and psychiatric savings use Forget's 8.5% on person-years lifted above the cost of living; emergency rooms only in the high end.
- **v5.0's main row** = session 30's release + steps 14-16. The readings shown beside it: H1, essentials backed, creative work at cost, the 5-year capacity rule, wage contribution, everyone joins, price cuts free, the former stand-ins, session 30's build, and v4.22. The "one split for every ESP" row is dropped: with the correction no private owner keeps the premium, so the row no longer describes an alternative.

## Session 35 (Oct 3, 2026): step 3, savings that keep up with prices, and the BU indexed every year

- **The interest rule.** Each year from year 1, every adult's savings (positive wealth) earn the year's price rise plus a real rate: savings × ((this year's price level ÷ last year's) × (1 + r) − 1), where the price level is the one the cost of living reads (outside inflation times the price module's basket index). It is a switch (`SAVE`, off by default; testbed row option `sv`), and like every mechanism that is not design-specific it applies to the no-programme run too: each reading that uses it is compared with a no-programme run under the same rule (`releaseBases`: rows paired by `bk`). Alternatives: interest on BU or on income (the item is about savings); a nominal interest rate (the point is protection from price rises).
- **The real rate, r = 0.97%.** The average real yield on 10-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities over every full year of the series, 2003-2025 (FRED DFII10, 5,753 daily observations; `sources/tips_real_rate.py`). Why the long-run mean: the runs last 20 or 40 years, so one year's yield (1.96% in 2025) would overstate or understate what savers earn over the run; why 10 years and not 5 (0.55% over the same years): closer to a saver's horizon. Reading beside it: r = 0 (savings that only keep their value). The two give almost the same results, which shows that what matters is the protection from inflation, not the real return.
- **Debts.** Left as they are by default (the floor at −$10,000 already caps them); a switch (`debt: 'same'`) lets them grow at the same rate. Why: the item is about savings, and charging indexed interest on debts would add a second change with its own question (who lends at those rates).
- **Timing and use.** The interest is added at the start of the year (it carries last year's savings into this year's prices) and is reinvested: it does not enter the year's income measures, the spending share or the labour response. Wealth reaches the rest of the model only where it already did: through the BLEI, which counts 20% of savings and gates the wage-growth bonus and PTF adoption, so cost-of-living poverty moves by a few hundredths of a point.
- **Who pays the interest: not modelled, and said so.** The model has no bank, so the interest is read as paid by borrowers and the economy outside the 500 adults, and it is not counted as new money in the price rule. This is the honest limit of the reading. In the release row the interest is mostly compensation for the programme's own inflation (about 46% a year in the Adverse Environment) and comes to about $47,000 per adult a year in today's dollars at 12 seeds, more than the programme's cost (about $27,500); only about $1,300 of it is above inflation. In this model the inflation tax on savings is how the Source's new money gets its real resources, so protecting savings fully means someone else would have to supply them. The reading therefore shows how much of the savings result is the missing protection of savings, and the page says it is not a version of the design that works as it stands. Alternative: count the interest as new money (it would feed the price rule and run away), or fund it from the Source (a design change the Hub does not describe). Both are left out; the size of the transfer is reported beside the reading (`svInt`, and `svIntR` for the part above inflation).
- **The BU indexed every year (Duke's answer d147).** A row option (`ci`) that sets the release row's indexing threshold to −1, so the BU follows prices every year; the Hub's rule (indexed only in a year prices rise faster than 5%) stays the main reading. At the cautious end it changes nothing at all (the programme's own inflation of 24-46% a year triggers the Hub's rule every year); it matters at H1, where outside inflation of 2% never triggers the rule: in the Adverse Environment at 12 seeds wealth poverty at H1 falls from 53.6% to 32.7%, at a cost of $37,085 instead of $25,506 per adult a year (the 500-seed check in session 34 gave 52.5% and 31.8%).
- **What the page shows.** The readings table gains the savings readings (labelled as compared with no programme under the same savings rule), the indexing reading, H1 with the BU indexed every year, and H1 with both. The front door adds one sentence under "Savings" on what savings that keep up with prices would do, with the size of the interest and who would have to pay it, and the optimistic-end sentence gives H1 with the BU indexed every year and its cost. The replication page gets a table of every reading added in this round.
- **How much of the Adverse and Stress gap each closes (12 seeds, 20 years; the 200-seed study is in `dev/runs/save-check-ENV.json`).** Release row against no programme, share with too little wealth: Adverse +3.8 points, Stress +6.8. With savings that keep up with prices in both runs: Adverse −40.2, Stress −14.6 (the gap is closed and reversed); with r = 0: −40.3 and −14.3. With the BU indexed every year: unchanged (+3.8, +6.8). ## Session 35 (Oct 5, 2026): step 6, a middle backing reading and idle workers in normal years

- **The question.** The page's two ends are "none of what the Source pays is backed by new output" (the release row: prices rise from the programme) and "all of it is" (H1: no price rise). The truth is probably between. Duke's decision B: show a middle reading as a labelled band beside the two ends, name the Kenya study and its limits, and keep the headline at the cautious end.
- **How the Kenya study anchors it.** Egger, Haushofer, Miguel, Niehaus and Walker (2022, *Econometrica* 90(6)) gave one-time cash transfers of about $1,000 to over 10,500 poor households in 653 randomly chosen villages in rural Kenya. The transfers came to about 16% of a year's local output in the peak 12 months and 24% over the 24-month rollout; local output rose (a local multiplier of 2.5) and prices rose only 0.1% on average (under 1% where transfers were largest). What the study measures is the size of a new flow of spending that an economy with idle capacity met with output rather than prices. So the reading carries over a size: new output backs the Source's net payout up to a share of the year's earned income (wages and Social Security; the programme's own payments are left out of the base, as Kenya's share is of the area's output); any payout above that is new money, as in the release row. **Middle: 12% a year** (the rollout's 24% over two years). **Band: 8% to 16%**: 16% is Kenya's peak year; 8% is all of the US's underused labour (BLS U-6, 2025: the unemployed, people who want work but stopped looking, and part-timers who want full-time work), the idle labour the US has to meet extra spending with. Row option `aK`; readings `mid`, `midlo`, `midhi`. Alternatives: a fixed backed share of one half (the middle of the existing curve; no anchor in evidence, and it treats a payout of any size alike); Kenya read literally as everything backed (that is H1, and it ignores that the payout here is three to four times larger relative to income, every year); a share set by the multiplier (a multiplier above 1 would mean everything backed, the same problem).
- **Why Kenya does not set the US number.** The transfers were paid once, not every year, so idle capacity was used once; they came from donors outside the area, not new money; the area is small within a currency union, with no response from a central bank or the exchange rate; the authors attribute the absorption to slack in one-person businesses that work "on demand", which a high-income economy has far less of; and the Source's payout here is 43% of earned income a year in the Reference Environment, 58% in the Adverse and 25% in the Stress Test (`bkPY`), against Kenya's 16% in the peak year. These are listed beside the reading on the page, and the headline stays at the cautious end.
- **Idle workers in normal years (the spending layer).** Until now the spending layer put idle workers to work only in recessions. In normal years the US also has idle labour: U-6 was 8.0% in 2025 (BLS, 11-month average; October was not collected), and its lowest annual level since the series began in 1994 is 6.9% (2022 and 2023; BLS series LNS13327709 via the DBnomics mirror, since FRED refused this environment's connections on Oct 5). **Main reading: the idle labour spending can fill is U-6 above its lowest level, 1.1% of wages** (the part that has come and gone with demand; the rest is people between jobs and others whom more spending does not reach). Upper bound: all of U-6, 8%. **Multiplier 0.6**: Ramey and Zubairy (2018, *Journal of Political Economy*; NBER WP 20719) find most estimates between 0.6 and 1 with no difference between slack and normal times; the low end is taken. Auerbach and Gorodnichenko (2012) find about zero in expansions, which is the layer switched off (the release row). The wages added = the smaller of (idle share × the wage bill) and (labour share 0.508 × multiplier × last year's net Source payout); they reach every worker in proportion to the wage, as the recession layer does, in years without a recession only (in a recession the recession layer already counts the idle labour). The no-programme run has no programme spending, so nothing changes there. Row option `ml: {nt: {m, s}}`; readings `slack`, `slacku6`.

- **Landlords raising rents (housing capture).** The question: if BU pay rent, do landlords take part of it, as studies of US housing vouchers found? Two sources, read the way each measured it. Collinson and Ganong (2018, *AEJ: Economic Policy* 10(2)): each $1 rise in the voucher rent ceiling raised voucher holders' rents by about $0.50, with little or no better housing; so the main reading raises the rent of tenants who pay with BU by $0.50 per BU dollar spent on rent. ## Session 39 (Oct 6, 2026): v5.3 B2, the accounting identities as checks

- **The adults stay the model's agents.** The question: make the household the agent, or group the existing adults into households. Chosen: group them. The 500 adults keep every latent trait and their eight random draws a year in the same order, so with households off nothing changes and with households on every adult is still paired with the same adult in the no-programme run. Children are dependants of a household (an age each), not agents. Alternative (households as agents): every draw and pairing would change, and the adult-only results could not be compared.
- **Who lives with whom.** Drawn once at the start on a new random stream (seed + 500009), as fixed counts so every run has the CPS mix: of adults 25-64, 31.6% in a couple with children under 18, 32.9% in a couple without, 5.0% single parents, 30.5% single without children (adults living with parents, adult children or roommates count as single budgets: a stated simplification). Partners are paired at random (no matching on wages: a modelling assumption that understates how unequal households are; CPS gives no earnings correlation of partners, so matching is left for a sourced reading). Children: 1, 2, 3 or 4+ by the parent's arrangement (A3; 4+ counted as 4); each child's age from the age spread of children living with two parents or one (C2), uniform within under 6, 6-11 and 12-17.
- **What a household needs.** MIT's budget for its family type (one or two working adults; children capped at MIT's three), each component evaluated at the model's $49,370 one-adult level by the same regression that gives `CFG.BASKET` (it reproduces those shares). Childcare is its own component: MIT's own prices in MIT's own order (first child $12,914, second $11,638, third and more $7,824 at the model's level), for children under 13 only. This matches MIT's one- and two-child budgets exactly at MIT's assumed ages (4 and 9) and stops care at 13, as the design note says; MIT prices a third child aged 15 at school-age care, which the model does not. Couples use MIT's "both adults working" budgets (every adult under retirement age earns in the model).
- **Prices.** Childcare is priced with the general price level (it is not a BU purchase under the present BU scope); every other component keeps its own price index, so a household's cost moves with its own mix.
- **Each adult's share.** An adult carries an equal share of the household's budget and of the children's BU allowance (each child: `childBU` × the adult BU, Duke's quarter by default, swept 0 to half). Under either pooling rule the shares are the same; pooling decides what happens to the money (below).
- **Pooling (d168).** 'household' (main reading): at the end of each year a couple's surplus is pooled before the spending rule takes its share, their wealth is shared equally, and the debt floor applies to the household; poverty status is the household's (household income against household cost). 'individual' (reading): each adult pays their equal share of the costs from their own income and keeps their own savings and floor. Done as a short end-of-year step per couple, so no adult's random draws move.
- **Decisions taken together.** Taking part, PTF membership and PTH membership are the household's: a partner takes the first adult's draws at the start, and later joins (open enrolment, PTF adoption) follow the first adult's decision. Alternative (each adult decides): couples would split across programmes, which the Hub's household framing does not suggest.
- **BLEI for a household.** The BLEI paper's daily cost of basic living is rent, food, utilities and transport (its section 1); a household's daily cost is the one-adult $68.33 scaled by MIT's (housing + food + transport) for the household against one adult's, and each adult's BLEI uses their own resources against an equal share of it (so the household's BLEI is the mean of its adults'). Children's needs enter through the household's cost.
- **Scope.** Households work in the framework (Hub-spec) conversion model, the release model; the earlier engine model ignores the switch. BU scope ('hub': transport and education, childcare at PTF providers) and the poverty lines by household size follow in the next sub-steps.
- **Built (B3b-c).** As designed, with three things settled while building: (1) the no-programme run is a household economy too (households are part of the population, not of the programme), and so is the price model's reference path for essentials (the no-programme demand path it compares against is computed with the same households); (2) the poverty status of a pooled couple comes from an end-of-year step after pooling, through the same function as everyone else's (`tbPov`); (3) households and ageing cannot yet be combined (the run stops with a message): who replaces an adult who dies, and what happens to a partner and children, is B4 and B5.
- **B3d: the household measures.** Every person counts, children included, with their household's status (its resources against its needs, under either pooling rule; the adult measures keep each adult's own status under 'individual'): below the cost of living, below the official poverty line for the household's size and children (Census 2025 thresholds, `CFG.HH_FPL`, moved with prices, on money income before tax as the official measure counts it), too little wealth, and below 30 days of basic living (the household's BLEI, the mean of its adults'), as shares of persons and of children (child poverty), person-years and Year 7 and the last year; the same by household type; and the Gini and median of disposable income per equivalent adult (household income over the square root of its size, the OECD's square-root scale, as the design note says), across persons. Alternatives: count adults only (children, the point of the round, would be invisible); the OECD-modified scale (1 + 0.5 per further adult + 0.3 per child; the square-root scale is the OECD's current default for comparisons and needs no age split). A test checks that with every adult living alone the household measures equal the adult ones exactly.

It is within 1.3% of six months of one adult's MIT cost of living ($24,685). Chosen: relabel it a design threshold with that stated reading, keep $25,000 for the adult measures so they stay comparable across releases, and give households the same rule (net wealth below six months of the household's own cost of living, `CFG.HH_WEALTH_MONTHS`). Alternatives: replace it with the asset-poverty line of the literature (net worth below three months of the poverty line, Haveman and Wolff 2004: $4,187 for one adult in 2025; far lower, and a different question: emergency cushion against destitution, not security at the cost of living); keep it unexplained.

## Session 39 (Oct 7, 2026): v5.3 B4 and B5, children's lives and estates (households with ageing)

These settle how households live through a run when ageing is on (the v5.2 ageing reading: adults age, retire at 67 and are replaced by a new 25-year-old when they die). Everything here is read only when households and ageing are both on; either off leaves every run as it was (proved by a full-output comparison of every household row and both adult ageing rows, three environments, and by the engine lineage). Sources and derivations: `sources/households.py` section 6 (Census CPS ASEC 2023 Table FG3; Census Vintage 2024 population by sex; tables A1, A3, C2, UC3), `sources/births_us.md` (NCHS 2024 birth rates by the mother's age; CPS 2025 Table AD-1).

- **When a child leaves home.** Chosen: a child stops being a dependant at 18 and leaves the household; from 18 to 24 they are outside the model; at 25 they can enter it as an adult forming their own household. Why: the model's adults start at 25 (the age range every calibration describes), and people aged 18 to 24 have never been in the model; Census AD-1 (2025) shows 57.6% of 18-24-year-olds still at home (college dormitories counted) and 16.4% of 25-34-year-olds, so by the model's entry age most have left, and those who have not are treated as their own budget, as B3 treats every adult 25-64 living with parents. Alternatives: keep children at home until a sourced median leaving age, about 22 or 23 by AD-1 (rejected: an 18-24-year-old at home would need an income, a basket and, under the draft Act, an adult BU, none of which the model has); bring them in as adults at 18 (rejected: no wage or wealth calibration below 25). Stated limit: households lose an 18-year-old's costs at once, which understates the costs of families with young adults at home.
- **Who takes the place of an adult who dies.** Chosen: a grown child of a model household turning 25 that year, drawn at random among them if more than one is waiting; otherwise an unrelated 25-year-old, as in v5.2. Grown children who find no place leave the model, so the population stays at its size. Why: this keeps v5.2's ageing design (a fixed population, replaced only at death) and every per-adult measure and price-model total it rests on, and follows the plan ("in place of the unrelated 25-year-old entrants"). The newcomer is drawn exactly like an unrelated one (wage, wealth, traits), so the only difference is the family link, which estates use. Alternatives: let every grown child enter (the population would grow by about 10% over 20 years, and every per-adult total in the price model would need re-normalising: a larger change than this step); let grown children wait for a place (they would enter older than the model's entry age). Stated limit: no inheritance of earnings (in the US the rank-rank correlation of parents' and children's incomes is about 0.34, Chetty, Hendren, Kline and Saez 2014).
- **Ages within households.** Chosen: a mother's age given her children's ages is drawn in proportion to the 2024 population at each age times the 2024 birth rate at the age she would have had each child; the partner's age adds the spouses' age gap from FG3 (with or without children; redrawn while outside 25-66); a childless couple's woman follows FG3's wives without children; single adults follow the CPS singles by age; single parents are mothers with C2's share (83.2%). Why: births depend strongly on the mother's age, and the adult-only ageing reading's independent ages would give, for example, 64-year-old mothers of toddlers. Check (seeds 1-3): spouses' mean gap 1.9 years against FG3's 2.1. Alternative: the CPS Table A3 parents by age (rejected as the main input: it mixes both parents and cannot be matched to the children's ages; kept as a check).
- **Births.** Chosen: each woman up to 49 gives birth with the 2024 rate at her age (NCHS) times 1.24 with a partner present and 0.60 without. The two multiples are derived, not fitted to model output: women 25-49 as a whole give birth at the national rates, and 22.2% of births go to women without a partner, the CPS share of children under 6 who live with one parent (C2). That share also stands in for young families that split, which the model cannot represent. Why: with the same rate for every woman, 36% of births would go to women without a partner at the start, against 22% in the data. Alternatives: the same rate for all; NCHS's rates for unmarried women (rejected: "unmarried" includes cohabiting couples, which the model counts as couples). Check: in year 1 the model has 7.1 births per 500 adults, 21% of them to women without a partner.
- **Children whose last parent dies** leave the model (to relatives outside it), counted. Alternative: the newcomer takes them in (no basis).
- **Estates (B5, Duke's d169).** As decided: to the surviving partner, else to the children in equal shares, else the estate leaves the model. Settled while building: the estate is positive wealth and Acre Equity (debts are not inherited; BU held for later conversion lapse, as since B2); children under 18 count as heirs, and their shares leave the model with them; a grown child's share reaches them only if they are an adult in the model; Acre Equity stays Acre Equity for an heir in community housing, otherwise it is paid at the deceased's liquid share and the rest stays with the trust (Claude's reading of the Hub's "Inheritance Rights", design note 1.5). Estates pass at the start of the year, before the year's accounts open, so the yearly identities are untouched. `estate 'leave'` (every estate leaves the model, the adult-only rule) is the comparison.
- **The price model's reference path** for a row with households and ageing is the no-programme economy of the households as drawn, without ageing, as the v5.2 ageing rows' reference path had no ageing.
- **Finding: with partnerships fixed, families drift over 20 years.** The plan keeps partnerships as first drawn this round (no new couples, no separations). With ageing, that has a large effect: the starting couples age past childbearing and every newcomer arrives single, so in Reference (8 seeds) women up to 49 with a partner fall from 100 to 14 per 500 adults by year 19, births from 7.1 to about 2 a year, the share of births to women without a partner rises from 21% to about 80%, and children fall from 186 to 56. Part of this is the v5.2 ageing design itself (only deaths are replaced, so the population ages: mean age 45 to 57). Kept as the plan says and reported plainly; the children's figures of this reading describe the drawn families followed over time, not a representative population in later years. A fix needs partnership formation and separation from sourced rates, recorded as a candidate for a later round. The main result has no ageing and is not affected.

## Session 39 (Oct 7, 2026): v5.3 B6, household income shocks and a measured EDC

- **How partners' incomes move together.** The model's income shock is the year's macro multiplier (the recession path, common to everyone) times each adult's own yearly swing (uniform 0.90 to 1.10, drawn on the main stream; an earlier version's spread, with no recorded source, listed as a limit). Chosen: with households, a couple's two swings can share a household part drawn once a year on its own stream (seed + 500041), mixed so that their correlation is the chosen value and each adult's swing keeps exactly its spread (no draw added to the main stream). The value comes from Shore (2010, "For Better, For Worse: Intrahousehold Risk-Sharing over the Business Cycle", Review of Economics and Statistics 92(3): 536-548): spouses' labour-income changes correlate about +0.10 in good years and -0.10 in bad ones (`CFG.HH_SHOCK_CORR`; the model's recession years are the bad ones), the 'shore' setting. Off (0) by default, so every reading built so far is unchanged; the v5.3 restudy's household rows use 'shore'; 0.5 is the stress reading in which partners lose income together. Alternative: a large common household shock, as the plan's wording suggests ("so partners can lose income together"; rejected as the main setting: the evidence finds spouses' income changes nearly uncorrelated, and negatively so in bad times, when the other partner works more). Losing a job together is a different risk, the co-movement of couples' employment (Juhn and Potter 2007), and arrives with employment states in v5.4. Expected effect: small, since the swing it reshapes is ±10%.
- **EDC from flows.** The plan asks for the Extractive Drain Coefficient measured from the model's flows and for the current formula to be labelled "design target proxy". Chosen, following the page's own glossary ("Share of income captured by contracts that build zero household wealth (rent, interest, fees). PTH payments have EDC = 0."): rent paid to a landlord (MIT's housing figure, which includes utilities, so this overstates rent slightly; a community-housing payment counts 0) plus interest paid on debt (only where the savings reading charges it; the model has no fees), over gross income including the BU allowance at face value (BU pay rent too, so leaving them out could put the ratio above 1). With households it is the household's ratio, counted for every person. Reported beside the proxy, never in its place; reporting only (switching it on changes no result: tested). Relabelled as "design target proxy": the earlier engine page's EDC tile, its scenario table and notes, the research export, and the glossary on both pages. The replication page's FBS formula now says that results using the 0.025 residual are conditional on PTH reaching that target. Alternatives: count community-housing payments as extractive (rejected: the glossary defines them as 0; the restudy can report the PTH payments separately); leave BU out of income (rejected, as above).
- **First look (Reference, 2 runs).** With no programme the measured EDC is 34% of adults' income, against 49% from the proxy; with the programme, 20% measured against 11% from the proxy. The proxy assumes most of the drain disappears; the flows show it halves. The restudy reports both.
- **A check's scale corrected (found by the 500-run children's-lives study).** In the Adverse environment the check that a couple's pooling transfers sum to zero flagged 11 of 4.56 million couple-years, each a gap of 2^-29 dollars (about two billionths), on couples whose wealth was about $8 million in that environment's late, inflated prices. The check had scaled its tolerance by the transfers alone, which are tiny when partners' wealth is nearly equal, while the rounding is relative to the wealth itself. Corrected: the scale is the transfers plus the wealth pooled, as every other check scales by the amounts it balances (the B2 rule: a billionth of the amounts being balanced). This is the check's definition, not a looser tolerance: a real gap of even a cent would still fail. No result changes (the check reads nothing that moves a result; lineage link 5776173074d3 -> 0b5f4974268a).

## Session 39 (Oct 7, 2026): v5.3 B7, four calibration readings

The plan asks for four calibrations, each first as a labelled reading. Each is off by default (every earlier result is reproduced exactly with it off), adds no random draw, and is applied in the no-programme run too (it describes the population, not the programme), so each comparison stays paired.

Why: the model's own draw gives each adult a median of $36,316, so a couple holds about $80,000, while the survey's median couple with children holds $272,609 (the 5.3x gap the audit found for the median). Single adults use the same table as the existing singles reading (`LATENT.wealth`), which this replaces for households (the two cannot be combined; the run stops if asked to). Check (seeds 1-20): starting medians within 9% of the survey for every type, correlations within 0.02.

Chosen: when an adult's income this year (wages, Social Security, a cash grant) is below their cash cost, they spend cost x (income / cost)^0.5573 and keep the rest. 0.5573 is the income elasticity of spending in the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey 2024 (quintiles 2-5, per equivalent adult; `sources/ce_spending.py`, the series read through FRED because bls.gov refuses scripted downloads); the lowest quintile, which spends twice its reported income, is left out of the slope (the CE's own documentation notes transfers, savings drawn down and under-reported income there). The anchor, full spending where income equals the cost, is a modelling assumption. The poverty measures still compare income with the full cost, so the cut is hardship and never counts as relief; the cut stays in the adult's wealth as its own accounted flow, and counts in the price model as basket consumption that did not happen (as a write-off at the debt floor does) but not as money. A pooled couple is graded on its totals. The price model's no-programme reference path uses the same rule. Alternatives: a consumption floor or a buffer-stock rule (rejected: no source at hand for its parameters); grading by total resources including BU (rejected for now: BU arrive through the year, after spending is set; with the programme most people's cash costs are covered by BU, so the rule rarely binds there anyway).
- **Earnings by age (`AGE.earn 'cps'`, with ageing).** Chosen: wages follow the shape of US median weekly earnings by age (BLS, CPS, Table 3 of the quarterly release, Q2 2026; `sources/earnings_age.md`): straight lines between the age groups' middle ages, normalised so the average over the population aged 25-66 is exactly 1 (a 25-year-old earns 0.73 of the average, a 45-year-old 1.08, a 65-year-old 0.97). At the start each adult's wage is multiplied by the curve at their age; each birthday moves it along the curve; a newcomer starts at the curve's value at 25; the wage index newcomers follow leaves the curve out. Alternative: the CPS ASEC's annual earnings by age (rejected for now: bls.gov and census.gov tables give the same shape, and only the shape is used). The 65-and-over group is placed at 67.5 (open band: a modelling assumption).
- **FBS50 (`FBS50`, row option `fb`).** The advancement rule's coefficient lambda (1/USD) is hard to read; FBS50 = ln 2 / lambda is the monthly free bandwidth surplus at which the chance of advancing is even ($524 to $4,191 over the model's range, exposed since v4.6). Chosen: a reading that reports, for every participant-year, whether their surplus reaches their own FBS50 (overall and for adults in households with children), with two options: `dist 'fbs50'` spreads FBS50 evenly over the same range (the same uniform draw read in FBS50 rather than in lambda: more adults need a larger surplus), and `scale` multiplies every FBS50 (a sweep: how hard advancing is). With lambda kept and scale 1, every result is identical (it only reports). First look (4 runs, Reference): 90% of participant-years are at or above their FBS50; spreading FBS50 evenly or doubling it moves headline figures by under half a point, because advancement nearly saturates over 20 years (mean octave 5.99 at the end) whatever the spread. The empirical grounding of lambda stays open, as the replication page says.

## Session 40 (Oct 7, 2026): v5.4 labour and markets, the design (before code)

The full reasoning is in `dev/reports/v5-21-v54-design.md`. The modelling choices, in short:

- **Job loss replaces automation as a pay cut (`EMPL`).** Chosen: four states (employed, displaced, searching, re-employed), displacement odds from the BLS Displaced Worker Survey, spell lengths from the CPS, pay loss on return from Farber (2017); the automation wave's existing schedule becomes extra displacement odds for high-risk occupations. Alternatives: keep the pay cut (kept as a labelled row); a monthly time step (rejected: the model is yearly; a spell is read as a share of the year). Why: the plan asks for it, and a pay cut spread over everyone hides that job loss hits a few people hard.
- **Unemployment insurance in both runs.** Chosen: the US's, at the measured recipiency and replacement rates. Alternative: none (unfair to the no-programme run, which would face job loss with no cushion the US has).
- **No extra effect of BU on job-search length.** Chosen: none beyond the existing income effect on hours (standing rule 7: one income effect per unconditional dollar). Alternative: a search-length elasticity from the UI literature (rejected: it would be a second income effect on the same dollar).
- **Occupation as the common cause of pay, risk and hours.** Chosen: 22 BLS major groups. Alternative: the existing pay-risk copula (kept as a labelled row). Why: the plan asks for it, and it ties three things to one measured cause instead of a fitted correlation.
- **PTH balance sheet (`PTHB`).** Chosen: trust accounts (homes as building and land, debt, members' and community equity) with an enforced identity; the Hub's inflation + 2% cap and 40% exit value as defaults (Duke's questions 1 and 2); exits at the renters' moving rate. Alternative: keep the member-side-only accounts (kept as a labelled row).
- **PTF by sector (`PTFS`).** Chosen: five sectors with their own capacity; Hub ε for food and utilities; no cut (cautious) for transport, health care and childcare, swept to 12% and 25%; leaving by moves out of county and by the open-enrolment rule. Alternative: the flat 12% (kept as a labelled row).
- **Sensitivity.** Chosen: Morris screening, then Sobol on the leaders, 10 paired seeds per point, Reference first. Alternative: one-at-a-time sweeps (rejected: they miss interactions).

## Session 40 (Oct 7, 2026): v5.4 build choices (items 1-5)

- **Risk measures (`RISK`).** Drawdown in months of basic living (wealth) and in BLEI days; an "income shock" is any year with real cash income below 80% of the year before, whatever the cause, so job loss, recessions and disasters are all caught by one rule; recovery = back to the year-before level; re-entry over exits with k years observed. Alternative: shocks by cause only (rejected: causes differ by switch; one rule compares them all). Reporting only.
- **Job-loss rates from the CPS by steady-state flows** (`sources/unemp_flows.py`): the yearly chance 0.085 (0.142 in 2009-10) counts only job losers and leavers (the model has no one entering from outside the labour force), so the model's unemployment (about 2.5% of adult-years in Reference) is below the official rate (4.2%): a stated limit, not tuned. Spell lengths from the duration counts (median about 9 weeks; 15% of normal-year spells and 30% of recession spells over 26 weeks).
- **Unemployment insurance**: 32% of pay (DOL average weekly benefit over average covered wage) for up to 26 weeks, for 27% of spells (continued claims over unemployed; 36% in 2009-10). Taxes on benefits not modelled.
- **The labour-supply response scales with the share of the year at work.** Hours already at zero cannot fall further; this keeps one income effect per dollar for time at work.
- **Recessions with job loss**: the recession's across-the-board income cut is reduced by the extra share of the year recession job loss takes on average (about 4.2 points), so the loss is not counted twice; 'add' kept as a reading. In the programme run the spending layer refills recession losses either way.
- **Occupations**: 700 Frey and Osborne occupations, assigned by pay rank with correlation sqrt(between-occupation variance of log pay / the run's variance). Finding: the data's between-occupation variance (0.265) exceeds the model's whole spread of log pay, so the correlation is 1 (occupation follows pay rank exactly): the model's pay spread is narrower than the data's (a stated limit carried from earlier rounds).
- **PTH balance sheet (`PTHB`)**: home value = 18.2 years of market housing cost (Fed Z.1 owner-occupied real estate over BEA imputed rent, 2015-2025); land 37.7%; upkeep 2.31% of the structure (BEA depreciation); property tax 0.65% (BEA); real house-price growth 1.72% (FHFA over CPI-U, 1991-2025); debt at 1.80% real plus the year's price rise (the 30-year rate less CPI inflation, 2015-2025), so inflation does not erase the trust's debt for free (the cautious choice). The trust receives the market housing cost (member's fee plus the programme's payment for the cut, already in the programme's cost). Cash paid out of appreciation is the existing liquid share applied to the gain above the price rise only (otherwise inflation would turn equity into spending; at the programme's inflation the old rule drained members' equity). Exits at renters' moves to another county (6.2%); leavers' homes go to the next adult by uptake draw.
- **PTF by sector (`PTFS`)**: food and utilities from the Hub's epsilons (62% and 44% off), the food epsilon falling to 1.65 below 40% membership (the Hub's own words); utilities 12.8% of housing (BEA 2025); no cut in transport, health care and childcare at the main value (Hub silent; swept). Leaving only by moves to another county (3.2%); no value-based leaving because membership has no fee (the design note's two-year rule is dropped for that reason). Capacity: the existing membership gate; no sector capacity data.
- **Disaster (`DIS`)**: Year 7, 25% of households, $10,000 loss and 4 weeks' pay (design, swept); FEMA's mean award $3,092 for 50.6% of households in both runs; the programme adds 20% of the year's BU for hit participants (the Recession Protocol's figure; the disaster protocol gives none).
