Compassionism aspires to enhance cultures by eradicating extreme poverty while incentivizing participation and contribution: see how it does on poverty and work against five other designs on the same simulated people.

An open agent-based model runs six anti-poverty designs on the same 500 simulated adults for 20 years, under one set of rules: every design is paid for by a flat contribution on wages, set each year to cover its cost, and adults adjust how much they work by the same rules under every design. The model measures poverty, cost and work, not the cultural effects Compassionism aspires to. Poverty is measured against a living-wage basket, a higher bar than extreme poverty, with the deepest shortfalls weighted most. Compare the designs at equal cost, where the basic income, negative income tax and asset endowment cost exactly what Compassionism costs, or at the size their proponents propose; a public grocery network and X-Cents run at their own cost in both. . Compassionism's own scenarios also run live in your browser.

What each design is, and where it comes from

All six designs run on the same 500 simulated single adults for 20 years, under one set of rules. Each is paid for by the same flat contribution on wages, and every dollar of support that does not depend on work reduces earnings by the same 16 cents, whatever the design (the rate measured in the study cited under the basic income). The sources describe each real-world proposal; the model's version is simpler, and each entry says how. The table has two views. At equal cost, the basic income, negative income tax and endowment are sized to cost what Compassionism costs. At proposed size, each is set where its proponents set it. Designs of different sizes are not a fair contest by themselves, since the one that spends more usually cuts more, so every row also shows its change in poverty severity per $1,000.

No program (Baseline)

The same simulated adults with no anti-poverty program at all. It is not a model of current US programs such as SNAP or the Earned Income Tax Credit, which the simulation does not include. Every other row is measured against it.

Compassionism

The framework this simulation was built to study. Each participant receives a monthly allowance of Basic Units (BU), a restricted currency that buys essentials and expires if it is not used. BU can be converted to dollars at elevated rates, set by market demand and by the quality of the work as the community validates it through creative collectives (the multiplier rate). A participant's octave is their conversion capacity: a safeguard against exploitation, tied to what a business can carry, and an open ceiling for creators whose work draws demand, as a band that sells out a stadium outgrows a small hall. Wage work is still paid in dollars by employers; creative projects may also hire participants at elevated conversion rates, paid in expired BU. Community-owned businesses and housing (PTF, PTH) make the local economy more stable and lower living costs, zone coordination (SZH) adds a cooperative community benefit, and a civic internet portal (CIP) operates the currency and the votes. The allowance can be adjusted by region, by age (children might receive a small one) and for labor-market needs, such as supporting the workers a remote, cold-weather region depends on; the model gives every participant the same amount. Participation is open to everyone: any adult can choose to receive BU and contribute work, so not taking part is a choice, not an exclusion. In the model, 78% of adults take part at the reference settings and 40% in the Stress Test; each adult's choice is set at the start and kept for 20 years, so an adult who would gain by joining later cannot.

What sets it apart from the other designs is that they add dollars to the existing economy, or lower some of its prices. Compassionism creates both a supply of a new restricted currency (the monthly allowance) and a demand for it (conversion at elevated rates rewards the work a community values, and expired BU flow to the projects participants choose to support). Where current policy manages inflation through the money supply and interest rates, the design aims first to hold the cost of basic living steady through community-owned supply, and then to let competition and public policy aimed at human flourishing bring prices down. It has no precedent at scale, and a simulation can represent only part of it: the model has no firms and no production side, so it cannot show whether conversion rewards pay for new output, or whether new products and services raise the currency's value, as the framework expects. What it can show is how the allowance, the price cuts and the conversion rewards change poverty, work and cost for the same people, under the same rules, as the other designs.

The table offers two versions. Coded in the engine is the simulation as it runs live on this page: each participant converts part of their own BU. Specified on the Hub follows the Research Hub's written design: businesses convert the BU they accept, expired BU go to projects that participants convert, and each participant's octave sets how much they may convert. It is estimated to cost about two and a half to three times as much per adult, depending on the environment; the framework holds that the culture it builds is worth far more than it costs, a return the model cannot measure.

The shaded row directly beneath Compassionism switches off two mechanisms that are theoretical, yet to be empirically tested, and cost nothing in the model. Both are the model's stand-ins, not the design itself. The first adds 0.3% a year to the wage growth of each adult for each octave they hold, standing in for the higher earnings the framework expects from the skills and standing gained in the collectives; in the design, the octave sets conversion capacity, not wages. The second slows general inflation for everyone in proportion to PTF and PTH membership, standing in for the design's aim of stabilizing the cost of basic living. Together they carry most of Compassionism's edge in the table, which is why both rows are always shown.

The next row adds a needs-based top-up at the same total cost: a tenth of the flat allowance is moved to extra BU for participants with low earnings, reduced by 50 cents for each dollar earned. The model treats the top-up like cash, since it is no larger than what those adults spend on essentials. Its note gives the same design with the two theoretical mechanisms off. Compare its share in poverty and hours with the Compassionism row as well: shrinking a benefit as earnings rise works like a tax on work.

Sources: Better To Best Research Hub (the framework papers); OSF archive, DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/QWTE2.

Basic income

The same cash payment to every adult, with no conditions and no means test. At equal cost it is set to cost exactly what the Compassionism row costs; at proposed size it pays $12,000 a year, the $1,000 a month paid in the study below. Like BU, it is raised for inflation only when prices rise more than 5% in a year.

The model's work response comes from the largest US study of unconditional cash, in which 1,000 low-income adults received $1,000 a month for three years. That study paid low-income adults only; a basic income pays everyone.

Source: Vivalt, Rhodes, Bartik, Broockman, Krause and Miller, “The Employment Effects of a Guaranteed Income: Experimental Evidence from Two U.S. States,” NBER Working Paper 32719.

Negative income tax

A guaranteed income that shrinks as earnings rise: an adult with no earnings receives the full guarantee, and each dollar earned reduces the payment by 50 cents until it reaches zero (the harness also tests 30 and 70 cents). Milton Friedman proposed it in 1962, and it was tested in US field experiments from the late 1960s. At equal cost the guarantee is set so the program costs the same as the Compassionism row; at proposed size it is the 2026 federal poverty guideline for one person, $15,960.

Because it concentrates money on the lowest incomes, it can cut the depth of poverty more per dollar than a flat payment. Its phase-out also reduces what people keep from each extra dollar earned, so compare the hours and the share-in-poverty columns as well. Compassionism's allowance is not means-tested; its needs-based top-up borrows this design's phase-out.

Source: Robert A. Moffitt, “The Negative Income Tax and the Evolution of U.S. Welfare Policy,” Journal of Economic Perspectives 17(3), 2003; the guarantee at proposed size, US Department of Health and Human Services, 2026 Poverty Guidelines.

Asset endowment

A one-time grant of wealth instead of a yearly payment. The model pays it once, in year 1, to every adult who starts with less than $25,000, sized so that its cost spread over the 20 years matches the Compassionism row; it earns no return. Because 20 years of budget is paid at once to the adults who qualify, each grant is large. Poverty counts it as income at its yearly value, the grant divided by 20. At proposed size it is the stakeholder grant below, $80,000 in 1999 dollars or $154,594 in 2025 dollars, paid once to adults who start with less than $25,000, since the model has no young adults. The grant earns no return in the model, which understates what an invested grant would do.

The nearest real proposals are the stakeholder grant (a one-time $80,000 to every young adult) and baby bonds (a publicly funded trust account for every child at birth, with more for poorer families). The model has no children, so this is not a baby bond.

Sources: Bruce Ackerman and Anne Alstott, The Stakeholder Society (Yale University Press, 1999); Darrick Hamilton and William Darity, “Can ‘Baby Bonds’ Eliminate the Racial Wealth Gap in Putative Post-Racial America?” Review of Black Political Economy 37, 2010.

Public grocery network

City-owned grocery stores that sell a core basket of staples below market prices to everyone, regardless of income. It is calibrated to New York City's plan, announced on July 27, 2026: five municipal stores, one per borough, selling a core basket 30% below typical retail prices, which the city projects will cut the average grocery bill of their shoppers by 15%. Those are the city's projections, not results; the first store is due to open by the end of 2027.

Five stores cannot serve a city of eight million. The Mayor puts the number of existing grocery stores at more than 1,000, so five stores of the same size would serve about 0.5% of shoppers, and the La Marqueta store is planned at 9,000 square feet, smaller than a typical supermarket. The table therefore shows the plan as announced, which gives 15% off food to about 0.5% of adults, and the network at full scale, with enough stores to give every adult 15% off the food part of the living-wage basket. Both include a placeholder for the stores' building cost. Five stores are too few to move the prices other grocers charge, and the model has no such effect at any scale.

Sources: NYC Mayor's Office, “Mayor Mamdani Unveils 30% Discount at New Municipal Grocery Stores” and its transcript (July 27, 2026); “Mayor Mamdani Announces La Marqueta as First Site” (April 2026).

X-Cents

CurrentSea X-Change (X-Cents) lets a community reassign value to the coins and bills it already holds, by agreement and without a new mint. It has three mechanisms: bills sealed in plastic that gain value with each local trade; the Power of 1, under which every coin counts as $1 for essentials during designated community hours; and daily coin exchanges with a weekly birth-year prize wheel.

The model represents two of them. In the adult daily exchange, each adult trades a dime for $10 once a day, a net $9.90, or $3,613.50 a year, paid as a flat cash transfer. For the Power of 1, with one designated day a week, adults buy their week's food with pennies on that day, so food costs one cent on the dollar; if they did not move their shopping to that day, the saving would be a seventh as large (tested in the harness). Rent and utilities paid the same way would need landlords and utilities to take part, so they appear only as a sensitivity row. Sealed bills and the children's exchanges need a network of local trades and children, which the model does not have. Like every design here, the model pays for X-Cents with a contribution on wages; in the framework, the merchants and community that agree to honor the new values carry that cost.

Sources: X-Cents (the framework's site); “CurrentSea X-Change: A Framework for Citizen-Led Monetary Reset Using Existing Currency Infrastructure” (Academia.edu).
