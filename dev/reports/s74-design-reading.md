# Claude's reading of the design: stability, essential markets and central banks (S74)

Written Nov 15, 2026, before any S74 pilot run, as the preregistration asks (research-update `prereg/2026-11-s74-central-bank.md`, "What is built", the paragraph after item 7). It records what the Research Hub's papers say, and where they are silent, the reading the model takes. It is a modelling note, not a finding.

## What the papers say
- **Price stability is a stated aim of the currency design.** The dual-currency paper (`dual-currency-inflation.html`) says Creative Currency Octaves "could achieve poverty reduction goals while maintaining price stability through sectoral demand isolation, velocity controls, and capacity-constrained conversion mechanisms". The integrated framework paper (`cco-ptf-integrated-framework.html`) describes the framework as aiming at "comprehensive poverty elimination and economic stabilization".
- **The Public Trust Foundations are described as a stabiliser through their portfolios.** The same paper says the PTFs give "economic stabilization through diversified asset portfolios following Alaska Permanent Fund principles", and cites Norway's sovereign fund. The PTH financing pages describe PTH investments as having "lower volatility than traditional real estate".
- **Household shocks are framed through the Basic Living Economic Index.** The BLEI paper ties its Precarious tier to Lusardi, Schneider and Tufano (2011): most US households could not absorb a $2,000 shock without borrowing. It names stochastic testing (income volatility, health shocks, job loss, housing downturns) as work that would strengthen the framework.
- **Central banks are barely discussed.** The papers mention central banks only as infrastructure (digital currency architectures, swap arrangements, cyber-resilience standards) and cite Taylor (2016) in the dual-currency paper's references. No paper describes how a central bank would respond to the programme, or says that the PTF and PTH share of essentials would shield households from monetary policy.

## Claude's reading (modelling assumptions where the text is silent)
1. **Which stability.** The papers mean two things: stable prices (the programme's own inflation must stay low) and households that can absorb shocks. The preregistered outcomes cover both: P1 (the spread of the basket's price level, split into the outside part and the programme's own) is price stability; P2, P3 and P5 (income swings, wealth drawdowns, and being pushed below the basket line by a shock) and P4 (recovery times) are household shock absorption. The BLEI paper's shock framing is already in the engine's risk measures: the fall in BLEI days (`rkDdB`, and the share of adults whose fall exceeds 30 days, `rkDdB30`). These two are added as secondary outcomes in Amendment 1, before the pilot. No other stability measure is named in the papers, so no other outcome is added.
2. **The central bank's role.** Because the papers are silent on monetary policy, the model does not assume that the design changes the central bank. The central bank follows the same rule with and without the programme. Whether the programme's share of essentials damps what the central bank does to households is tested (the interaction, P1-P5), not assumed.
3. **The PTF portfolio claim** (diversified holdings as a stabiliser) is not what this step tests: the engine's PTFs are producers of essentials, and their asset portfolios are not modelled. The report says so.
4. **The stated aim, in Duke's words** (the planning chat of Nov 13, 2026, recorded in research-update PLAN.md, S74): stability and prosperity rather than exploitation and extraction, with citizens collectively ensuring essential markets so that wealthy interests cannot exploit volatility by buying low and selling high. The model measures the parts it can: who sells and who buys real assets in downturns (forced sales, item 5), who gains and who pays from rate moves and purchases (item 4), and the stability outcomes above. Claims about power, governance and intent are not model outputs; the report describes the Federal Reserve's governance as it is and lets the results speak for the comparison.
