# The central bank in both runs: the 100-seed pilot

Nov 15, 2026. A pilot, not the study's result: it checks that the mechanisms work and shows their size before the forward sweep and the full report (`dev/reports/s74-central-bank.md`). Preregistration: research-update `prereg/2026-11-s74-central-bank.md` and its Amendment 1 (written after the build and before this run). Engine `5f11ceb`. Results are in compassionism-sim-results `s74-cb-5f11ceb`, and the full tables are in `dev/runs/s74-pilot/tables.md` (`node dev/tools/s74_pilot.js ENV 100`, then `node dev/tools/s74_table.js`).

**First, the plain statement the preregistration requires.** In Reference, Adverse and Stress the programme's own yearly price change is the larger source of price instability. Outside inflation is a fixed rate there (0% or 2%), so its spread is zero, and the programme's own spread is 0.2-0.5 points. In History (2005-2025), outside inflation swings more (a spread of 1.76 points) than the programme's own (0.39-0.50 points).

## What was run
Today's population was run for 20 years in Reference, Adverse and Stress, and for 21 years in a new History environment that follows the actual US path of 2005-2025 (inflation, home and share prices, the federal funds rate, recessions). Each run used the frozen v5.9 main row, 500 adults and seeds 1-100. Both designs were run (the full-coverage design and the opt-in design), each paired with a no-programme run on the same seeds. Every environment was run with no central bank and with the new policy-rate rule (a Taylor rule floored at zero, bond purchases at the floor, refinancing, asset prices and job loss moved by the rule's stance, and forced sales of real assets by households that run out of credit). History was also run on the actual rate path.

## What the pilot shows
- **The programme makes households steadier in the ways that matter most at the bottom, and less steady in income.** With the central bank on, both designs cut the share of households pushed below the basket line in a recession year. Full coverage: from 9.3% to 1.4% in History, and from 10.4% to 0.6% in Adverse. The opt-in design: to 5.7% and 5.6%. The full-coverage design also cuts the median household's largest fall in real wealth, by about $47,000 in History and $41,000 in Adverse. Both designs raise the year-to-year swing in real cash income, by about $13,000 for the full-coverage design and $2,000-2,700 for the opt-in design. This measure is in dollars, so larger incomes swing more in dollars; part 3 reads it as a share of income beside the dollar figure. Recovery after an income shock is quicker under the full-coverage design in History, Adverse and Reference, and slower under the opt-in design. All of these differences hold after Holm's correction.
- **The design's claim that its share of essentials damps what the central bank and outside shocks do to the cost of basic living is not supported, for either design.** The central bank barely moves the basket's price level in this engine. The rule changes rates, asset prices and job loss, not the price of essentials, so the interaction on P1 is zero to two decimals everywhere. This is a property of the model: the rule has no channel to essential prices. It is not evidence about the world.
- **The central bank does change who gains.** Without the programme, the rule raises wealth poverty in every environment (by 3.4 points in History and 0.6 in Adverse; the 95% intervals exclude zero). With the programme on, the rule's effect on basket and wealth poverty is near zero. The programme's own inflation is low under full backing, so the rule rarely has to respond to it.
- **Forced sales move real assets up the wealth ladder.** Without the programme, about 22% of adult-years in History, Adverse and Stress (18% in Reference) involve a forced sale. Sellers are mostly in the lower-middle wealth groups. Buyers are mostly the top two groups (about 37% of purchases each). The full-coverage design cuts the share of adult-years with a forced sale to under 1%.
- **The rule itself.** In Adverse, recessions take the rule to the zero floor in about 14% of years, with purchases peaking at about 22% of GDP (Gagnon et al.'s slope). In Reference, the rule sits at the floor in every year without the programme, because 0% inflation is below the 2% target. In History, the rule is tighter than the actual Federal Reserve on average (3.6% against the actual 1.8%), which raises unemployment by about 1.1 points. About two in three households refinance at least once over the run. This is high because households hold their wealth group's average portfolio (a limit of the asset-class model).

## History checks (checked, not tuned; nothing was changed after them)
These compare the no-programme run on the actual rate path with the data, over 2005-2025.
- The top 1% share of net worth rises 7.6 points (Distributional Financial Accounts: +4.6): **matches** (same direction, within half to double).
- The bottom 50% share falls 1.2 points (DFA: -0.13): **same direction, different size**.
- Basket poverty rises 10.3 points, while the official poverty rate fell 2.4 points: **does not match**. Wealth poverty rises 1.8 points: **does not match**. Against the anchored SPM over 2005-2012 (the years the history pack holds), basket poverty rises 8.1 points against 1.45: same direction, different size. The engine's basket line is the cost of essentials, which rose faster than CPI over these years, while the official line moves with CPI. Part 3 reports this miss in the limits.

## Limits already visible
The rule has no channel to essential prices, so the damping claim cannot show up in P1 in this engine. Recessions do not move asset prices without the rule, so the rule's easing raises prices that never fell. The creditor side keeps charging on inflation. Most households with a home hold some fixed-rate debt (group-average portfolios). All of these are stated in the preregistration's Amendment 1 or the decisions file and will be carried into part 3's report.
