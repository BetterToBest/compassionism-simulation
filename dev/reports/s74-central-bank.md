**First, the plain statement the preregistration requires.** In Reference, Adverse and Stress, the programme's own yearly price change is the larger source of price instability: outside inflation is a fixed rate there (0% or 2%), so its spread is zero, while the programme's own spread is 0.24 to 0.52 points. ## In short
- **The programme makes households steadier where it matters most at the bottom, and less steady in prices and in dollar incomes.** With the central bank on, both designs cut the share of households pushed below the basket line in a recession year (full coverage from about 9-10% to about 1-2%; the opt-in design to about 6-7%) and cut the median household's largest fall in real wealth. Both raise the spread of the basket's price level and the year-to-year swing in dollar income. Every one of these holds after Holm's correction, in every environment that has recessions.
- **The design's claim that its share of essentials damps what the central bank and outside shocks do to the cost of basic living is not supported, for either design.** The interaction on P1 is less than 0.01 point from zero everywhere. This is a property of the model (the rule has no channel to essential prices), not evidence about the world.
- **The central bank moves wealth up the ladder, and the programme shrinks that.** Without the programme, the rule raises the top 1%'s share of net worth by 0.45 points over 2005-2025 and lowers the bottom half's by 0.48 points, against no central bank (by 0.36 and 0.32 points against the actual Fed; corrected Nov 16). Its gains go mostly to the richest fifth, through asset prices, deposits and buying in forced sales; the middle fifths lose, through forced sales and job loss. With the full-coverage design on, the same rule moves them less (+0.30 and -0.15 points; corrected Nov 16).
- **Forced sales move real assets from households worth $37,000-$665,000 to households worth over $1.9 million.** Without the programme, about one adult-year in five involves a forced sale; the full-coverage design cuts that to about 1%.
- **The rule's response to the programme rarely matters,** because the programme's own inflation is low under full backing. Its effect on the programme's poverty gains is small and mixed: it mostly trims them in History and Reference and adds slightly to the cut in basket poverty in Adverse and Stress.
- **Official inflation understates what lower-income households faced, and the engine cannot see it.** BLS's research index by income fifth (supplied by Duke after the first report) rose 2.69% a year for the lowest-income fifth and 2.43% for the highest over December 2005 to December 2025, against 2.52% for CPI-U: by 2025 the lowest fifth's prices stood about 3% above what CPI-U implies, the highest fifth's about 2% below. The engine's own-inflation measure differs by only 0.01 points across fifths, and gives the same near-zero gap even on BLS's own spending shares, so the gap comes from spending the measure does not separate. The engine's figure (2.77% a year) is an upper bound, as stated; BLS's levels are lower.
- **The forward sweep** (20 seeds, 33-34 settings of the rule in each environment; rerun later with v5.9's own settings) changes none of these conclusions: the programme's effects keep their sign in every setting but one small case, and no setting makes the damping claim show. What moves most is the central bank's own effect on households without the programme, which depends on forced sales and the floating-debt spread, both design parameters.

## What was run
Today's population (500 adults, the 2022-based start) was run for 20 years in Reference, Adverse and Stress, and for 21 years in History, which follows the actual US path of 2005-2025: inflation, home and share prices, the federal funds rate and the Fed's purchases, recessions. Each run used the frozen v5.9 main row in both designs (full coverage and the opt-in design), each paired with a no-programme run on the same seeds 1-100. Every environment was run with no central bank and with the policy-rate rule: a Taylor rule floored at zero (Taylor 1993's weights as the Federal Reserve's page writes them), bond purchases sized by the shadow rate below the floor (Gagnon et al. 2011's slope), refinancing for households with at least 20% home equity (Di Maggio, Kermani and Palmer 2016), share and home prices moved by the rule's stance (Bernanke and Kuttner 2005; Williams 2015), job loss moved by the stance, and forced sales of real assets by households that run past their borrowing limit. History was also run on the actual rate path, as a third arm. The rule is the same with and without the programme: the model does not assume the design changes the central bank (Claude's reading of the design, `dev/reports/s74-design-reading.md`).

Part 3 added reporting (no figure changed: all 542,000 values the pilot saved reproduce exactly, `dev/tools/s74_repro.js`) and one reading, the spending response `cb.dm`, which only the sweep reads.

Read with the levels: the bottom fifth's swings and falls matter more to its security than the top's, and P2 is in dollars, so larger incomes swing more in dollars. **As a share of income** (Amendment 2; the median adult's spread of yearly income changes over its mean income), the full-coverage design makes incomes steadier in History (29.3% to 27.7%, -1.6 points, interval -1.8 to -1.3), no measurably different in Adverse (-0.3, interval -0.6 to +0.03), and less steady in Stress (+0.4, interval +0.05 to +0.7) and Reference (+0.7, +0.4 to +1.0); its second to fourth fifths get steadier in History and Adverse (about 1.2 to 2.5 points), its middle and fourth fifths in Stress, and none of them measurably in Reference; its poorest fifth gets steadier in History (-2.3) and Adverse (-1.1), with no measurable difference in Stress and Reference; its richest fifth is less steady outside History (+1.8 to +2.5; 95% intervals; a secondary outcome, so no Holm correction). The opt-in design makes incomes less steady as a share in Adverse (+0.8 points), steadier in Reference (-0.9) and no different in History and Stress; its poorest fifth is less steady in History (+1.0) and Adverse (+1.7), with no measurable difference in Stress and Reference.

**The interaction** (research rule 4: the programme's effect with the central bank on, minus with it off; Holm-adjusted). On P1 it is less than 0.01 point from zero in every environment and design. Where its interval excludes zero, it is in the wrong direction for the damping claim in History (opt-in design) and in Adverse and Stress (both designs), and in the damping direction only in Reference (-0.002 and -0.001 points), which has no shocks. **Decision rule 2 (the damping claim): not supported for either design.** Basket poverty does not get worse. The central bank barely moves the basket's price level in this engine because the rule changes rates, asset prices and job loss, not the price of essentials. The engine cannot test the claim as the design states it; this result says nothing about the world.

The interaction is large on P3 in Adverse and Stress (full coverage about -$22,000 and -$20,000; the opt-in design -$8,900 and -$3,400): without the programme, the rule creates the wealth falls it then shows. With no central bank, recessions in these environments do not move asset prices, so the median no-programme household's largest fall is about $240. With the rule, easing raises share and home prices in a recession and the later tightening lowers them, so the median fall is $43,056. The programme's growing savings outweigh much of those swings (the full-coverage design's median fall is $20,888). In History the interaction on P3 does not differ from zero (full coverage +$442, opt-in design +$359; both intervals include zero). On P5 the central bank adds a little to both designs' cuts in History (-0.2 and -0.1 points) and takes a little from them in Adverse (both designs) and Stress (both designs), by 0.07 to 0.2 points.

In History the rule is tighter than the actual Federal Reserve on average (a policy rate of 3.6% against 1.8%), so savers earn more on deposits and job loss rises (by about 1.1 points of unemployment). Its stance ends 2025 less tight against the actual Fed's than it began 2005, so share and home prices end higher, which the top holds most of. In the middle fifths the job-loss and forced-sale losses outweigh the deposit gains. The pattern (a small gain at the bottom, a loss in the middle, a large gain at the top) resembles the "mildly U-shaped" welfare gains Lee (New York Fed Staff Report 1108) finds for 2009-2015's unconventional policy, with the gain at the bottom coming from unemployment there and from deposits here; the model's channels differ from the paper's, and it gives no verdict on net inequality. The channels do not add to the total: the total also holds what the channels change later (saving out of the gains, the cost of debt carried on).

In shares of net worth (the end of the run; points; 95% intervals in the tables): the rule raises the top 1%'s share and lowers the bottom half's in History (+0.45 and -0.48 without the programme; +0.30 and -0.15 with the full-coverage design; +0.39 and -0.30 with the opt-in design) and in Reference without the programme (+0.07 and -0.20). In Adverse and Stress, where the rule eases in recessions, it lowers the top 1%'s share without the programme (-0.19) and with the opt-in design (-0.18 and -0.19); with the full-coverage design the fall is smaller and not distinguishable from zero (-0.10 and -0.12). The bottom half's share falls a little without the programme (-0.10) and with the opt-in design in Stress (-0.05), and does not measurably change with the full-coverage design or with the opt-in design in Adverse.

**Adverse, no programme (the rule against no central bank).** Everyone gains, and the gains rise steeply with wealth: +$786 for the poorest fifth, +$3,298, +$11,888, +$34,463, +$270,313 for the richest fifth, and +$4.0 million for the top 1%. The rule cuts the policy rate to the floor in recessions (14% of years) and buys bonds, so asset prices and job-loss income gain everywhere (job-loss income +$749 to +$1,769), while floating debt costs more (-$1,513 to -$5,455 in the lower four fifths) and forced sales move assets up (sellers in the second to fourth fifths lose $3,900-$6,400 of later gains; buyers in the top 1% gain $182,859). Stress gives the same no-programme figures (the two environments differ only for the programme).

**With the programme,** the rule's deposit channel dominates every group's gain (the full-coverage design's households hold large cash balances), forced sales almost stop, and the floating-debt cost falls to a few hundred dollars. In History with the full-coverage design, the rule adds $6,139 to the poorest fifth's real wealth and $10,261 to the middle fifth's (against $349,000-$354,000 for the richest fifth with either design).

## Forced sales and buying low
Without the programme, about 22.5% of adult-years in History, Adverse and Stress (18.6% in Reference) involve a forced sale. Most are not in recession years (30% of what is sold in History, 15% in Adverse; Reference has none): the trigger is floating debt growing past the borrowing limit, which the rule's floating-debt spread speeds up, and with no central bank there are no forced sales at all (they are part of the rule's reading). In History, 48% of what is sold comes from households worth $37,000-$197,000 and 39% from households worth $197,000-$665,000 (the top-tail study's wealth groups); 40% is bought by households worth $1.9-12.8 million and 25% by households worth more. Sales are at the year's price (no discount in the main reading; the source below puts a liquidity discount on a house at about 3-6%), so the transfer is in the later gains: a buyer gains $15,292 on average on what it bought by 2025. The full-coverage design cuts forced sales to 0.9-1.2% of adult-years in every environment; the opt-in design to 7.5% (History), 9.1% (Adverse), 16.9% (Stress) and 5.2% (Reference). This is the mechanism Duke's note describes ("buying low"): in this engine, the programme that most protects households from forced selling is the one that most reduces it.

**The discount, from its source (Amendment 3).** Campbell, Giglio and Pathak (2011; every Massachusetts house sale 1987-2009, supplied by Duke after the first report) find houses sold after a seller's bankruptcy fetch 3.4% less than comparable houses (6.2% with one seller), and foreclosed houses 27% less; the authors read the bankruptcy figure as the closest to a pure quick-sale discount, and tie part of the foreclosure figure to lower quality and damage. The engine's forced seller is an adult past its borrowing limit, nearest the bankruptcy case, so the discount's basis is now this source: 0.034 to 0.062 for a house, with 0.27 an upper bound. The main reading stays 0, as frozen; because the engine sells shares first and shares trade in a liquid market, its true discount lies between 0 and the house figure. Two readings were run (20 seeds, with the release's settings, `dev/runs/s77-fd/`; compassionism-sim-results `s74-fd-e5649ef`; every cell equals the corrected sweep's). In History, without the programme, the rule raises wealth poverty by 3.7 points at 0, 3.8 at 0.034 and 4.0 at 0.062 (Reference 1.6, 1.7, 1.8; Adverse and Stress 0.5, 0.7, 0.9), inside the range the 0.1 reading already gave (4.1). The programme's effects do not change (History, full coverage: basket poverty -42.6 points in all three; wealth poverty -28.4, -28.6, -28.7). No conclusion changes. (First run, without the release's settings: 3.5, 3.7, 3.9; full coverage -43.1 and -28.5 to -28.9.)

## Each household's own inflation
Over 2006-2025, CPI-U rose 2.54% a year on average. Pricing each household's basket at CPI-U's food, shelter and medical care, and the rest at the all-items index, gives 2.77% a year, with 10th to 90th percentiles of 2.75% and 2.78% and almost no difference by wealth fifth (2.76-2.77%; the top 1% 2.77%). Three cautions, and BLS's check. The rest is priced at the all-items index, which contains shelter and medical care; all three named components rose faster than all items over these years (food 2.60%, shelter 3.15%, medical care 2.95%), so the rest must have risen slower, and 2.77% is an upper bound. And the engine's basket shares differ only by household type (MIT's living-wage budgets), not by income, so the engine cannot see the differences BLS's research index by income fifth (R-CPI-I) measures. Nothing in the engine uses this measure.

**BLS's check (Amendment 3).** bls.gov refused R-CPI-I to this machine; Duke supplied the files on Nov 15, 2026, and the check was made afterwards, outside the engine (`dev/runs/s74-rcpi/rcpi.md`; `python3 dev/tools/s74_rcpi.py`). December 2005 to December 2025, % a year:

| | lowest-income fifth | second | middle | fourth | highest | lowest minus highest | CPI-U |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R-CPI-I | 2.69 | 2.64 | 2.55 | 2.50 | 2.43 | 0.26 | 2.52 |
| R-CPI-I chained (to Dec 2024) | 2.59 | 2.43 | 2.29 | 2.22 | 2.15 | 0.43 | 2.52 |
| the engine's method on BLS's 2005 shares | 2.83 | 2.83 | 2.82 | 2.82 | 2.82 | 0.01 | 2.52 |

Three readings. First, BLS measures what Duke's note describes: the lowest-income fifth's prices rose faster than the official average (0.17 points a year above CPI-U; the chained index, which allows for households switching between items, gives a wider gap), and the highest fifth's slower. Second, the engine's method cannot reproduce this even when given BLS's own shares of food, housing and medical care by income fifth: those shares differ too little between fifths, and the gap lies in the spending the method prices at the all-items index (transport, recreation, apparel, communication and the like) and in items inside the three groups. So the engine's near-equal own inflation across fifths is a limit of the measure, not a finding that households face the same inflation. Third, the method's level (2.82-2.83%) is above every fifth's R-CPI-I, which confirms that 2.77% is an upper bound. The engine's fifths are by wealth and BLS's by income, so the comparison is of size, not group for group. These are BLS's figures, not model outputs; nothing in the engine changes.

## How the Federal Reserve is governed (as it is)
The model's central bank is a rule, not an institution. The real one is governed as follows (pages opened Nov 16, 2026):
- **The Board of Governors** has seven members, nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, for fourteen-year terms; one term begins every two years. The Chair and Vice Chairs are nominated by the President from among the members and confirmed by the Senate, for four-year terms (federalreserve.gov, "Board Members").
- **The twelve Reserve Banks** each have a board of nine directors in three classes of three. Class A directors represent the member banks and Class B directors the public; both are elected by the member banks of the district. Class C directors represent the public and are appointed by the Board of Governors, which also names each board's chair and deputy chair from among them (federalreserve.gov, "Federal Reserve Bank Board of Directors"). So member banks elect six of the nine (St. Louis Fed, "Role of Bankers on Federal Reserve's Boards of Directors", 2022).
- **Reserve Bank presidents.** "As a result of Dodd-Frank, only Class B and Class C directors can appoint the presidents and first vice presidents of Reserve Banks. Class A directors, who represent banks, no longer have a role in the appointment of these officials" (New York Fed, press release of Dec 22, 2010). The St. Louis Fed adds that Class A directors may not sit on the search committees for these posts.
- **Policy.** The Federal Open Market Committee sets the policy rate: the seven governors, the New York Fed's president, and four of the other eleven presidents in rotation, each for one year (federalreserve.gov, "Structure of the Federal Reserve System").

Duke Johnson's view, as recorded in the plan (planning chat, Nov 13, 2026): decisions by an unelected board, and private banks' influence over it, shape millions of lives; quantitative easing supported Wall Street more than homeowners; official inflation is an average that misses what many households face; and citizens collectively ensuring essential markets would remove the volatility that wealthy interests can exploit by buying low and selling high. The design's stated aim, in his words, is stability and prosperity rather than exploitation and extraction. Claims about power, governance and intent are not model outputs. The model tests the parts it can measure, above: who gains and who pays from the rule's channels, who sells and who buys in downturns, how households' own inflation compares with the official average, and whether the programme's share of essentials steadies households.

*Nov 17, 2026: Duke Johnson's fuller wording.* His concern is not ordinary buying low and selling high but market cycles that can be engineered: bubbles, panics and crashes that let the wealthiest sell high and buy back low ("when there's blood in the streets, buy real estate"), and wars and false-flag events used the same way, while the news media, once owned by many, is now owned by a handful of companies that profit from the fear. The findings page carries this wording. These are his views, not model outputs; the model does not represent war, the media, or whether market swings are engineered.

## Limits (stated in advance, and found)
- Today's population lives through 2005-2025's paths. This is a model counterfactual, not a reconstruction of those years; only changes and differences are read.
- The rule is a reduced form: no bond market, no banks' balance sheets, no exchange rate. The responses of asset prices and unemployment are slopes taken from the papers (Bernanke and Kuttner 2005; Williams 2015), not derived in the model.
- **The rule has no channel to the price of essentials.** It moves rates, asset prices and job loss. So the test of whether the programme's share of essentials damps what the central bank does to the cost of basic living cannot come out positive in this engine. The null result on P1's interaction is a property of the model, not evidence about the world.
- Outside History, recessions do not move asset prices without the rule, so the rule's easing raises prices that never fell.
- Purchases are sized by the shadow rate and converted to a share of GDP at Gagnon et al.'s slope. In Adverse and Stress the recession's unemployment gap is large (unemployment rises 5.2 points, so the gap is -10.4%), so the rule's shadow rate goes deep below zero and the implied purchases are large: about 22% of GDP under the main values, and about 160% in the 2008- and 2020-style regimes (purchase response 1 with gap response 1), far beyond anything the Fed has done (its total assets peaked at about 37% of GDP at the end of 2021). Read those settings as bounds.
- The programme's own change in hours is not fed into the gap (a cautious modelling assumption), so the rule responds to the programme only through its inflation.
- Households hold their wealth group's average portfolio, so most households with a home hold some fixed-rate debt and some shares; refinancing reaches about two in three households at least once, which is high.
- The creditor side (`rt.cr`) keeps charging on the year's inflation under the rule; it no longer describes exactly who holds the gap between the rate and inflation.
- The job-loss channel counts each spell the rule adds or removes at its length and that year's pay, less unemployment insurance. It leaves out the lower pay on return and the spell's effect on saving. The forced-sale sellers' figure leaves out the interest the sale saved them on the debt it repaid.
- Each household's own inflation can differ only by household type, because the engine's basket shares come from MIT's living-wage budgets by household type, not from each household's spending. BLS's research index by income fifth (R-CPI-I, supplied by Duke) shows a gap of 0.26 points a year between the lowest- and highest-income fifths that the engine's measure cannot see, even on BLS's own shares (Amendment 3).
- The forced-sale discount applies to every real asset sold, shares included, while Campbell, Giglio and Pathak (2011) measure houses; at a house figure it overstates the discount on the engine's mix, which is sold shares first (Amendment 3).
- The PTF portfolio claim (diversified holdings as a stabiliser) is not tested here: the engine's PTFs produce essentials, and their asset portfolios are not modelled.
