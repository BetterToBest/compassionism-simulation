## In plain words (part 2: the results)
**The outside model's prices do not rise, and they cannot.** Given the same new money as the engine's C2.2 design (31% to 46% of household income a year), BeforeIT's consumer prices end within a few percent of its own run without the payout: +1.5% after 3 years (95% interval -0.3% to +3.2%) and -0.4% after 20 years (-7.1% to +6.8%), at full scale (8.9 million agents). The engine's prices are about 2.6 times their start after 3 years and about 408 times after 19 years. Its pass-through (the share of each percent of new money that ends up in prices) is 1 by construction; BeforeIT's is 0.00 (interval -0.01 to +0.01). By the preregistered rule, the outside engine's prices rise far less than the engine's, by a factor of about 400 after 20 years.

**But BeforeIT's zero is also an assumption, not a measurement.** Reading its code after the runs showed why its prices stay put. Each firm sets its price from its last price, its costs and the inflation it expects. In the packaged Austria every price starts at 1, so every firm's cost term is zero and stays zero, and all prices just grow by expected inflation. Expected inflation is a forecast from past inflation plus a random draw. The payout, demand, wages and bank deposits never enter it. Every one of the 1,365 runs confirms this to machine precision: the consumer price index grows each quarter by exactly the expected inflation, and the food, housing and medical indices equal it. So the two engines do not disagree about evidence. Each one's answer is fixed by its price rule: all new money passes into prices in the engine, none of it in BeforeIT. Neither measures how much would. The true figure for an injection this large is not known from either model.

**What the money does in BeforeIT instead:** real output rises about 15% within 3 years, because unemployment falls to zero and firms stretch each worker's output up to 1.5 times. Government debt grows from under 1 year of output to about 10 years of output after 20 years. When the same payout is paid from an income-tax surcharge instead, almost all of the output gain disappears (+0.8% after 3 years, against +15.5%), and prices are again unchanged. The release design, the Adverse and Stress amounts, counting the whole payout as new money, a central bank that answers Austria's own inflation, and doses of 1% to 30% of income all leave prices where they were, as the rule above says they must.

**What follows (preregistration rule 4):** the engine and its published figures stay as they are. Hub sentences about the programme's inflation should say the figure rests on the engine's placeholder rule (all unmatched new money reaches prices). They may say that a published outside model shows no price rise, but only together with the reason: its prices cannot answer demand. The engine's pass-through needs an outside source in the next engine round. This goes to the ledger as a decision for Duke.

## In plain words (part 1, as written before the results)
The Compassionism Simulation's prices rise fast under the programme. Under the C2.2 design (automatic BU at the cost of food, housing and medical care) the price level is about 410 times its starting level by year 20 in the Reference environment, and the figure is the same at 500 and at 50,000 adults (study A). This step asks an outside, published model of a whole economy what it does with the same money.

The engine's reason for the rise is one rule. Each year it counts the money the programme pays out that is not matched by new output, divides it by the year's cash income, and raises the general price level by that share. It is the quantity theory of money with a pass-through of 1, and the engine's own notes call it "a logged placeholder, swept 0.25-1". Under C2.2 the Source pays about $61,500 per adult a year in today's dollars (BU and conversion pay), the engine counts about 37% of it as matched by new output, and the rest, about $38,700, is new money: 30% to 50% of the year's cash income, every year. So prices rise by about that much each year.

The outside model is BeforeIT.jl, the Bank of Italy's open implementation of Poledna, Miess, Hommes and Rabitsch (2023), the first agent-based model of a whole economy to forecast as well as the standard models. It is calibrated to Austria and can run with one agent for every person and every firm (8.9 million agents). We add a module that pays every person the same share of household income as new money, year by year, as the engine's C2.2 design creates, and compare BeforeIT's price level with its own run without the payout, seed by seed.

What we already know from building it (before any study run, and stated in the preregistration so it is not read as a finding): BeforeIT's firms set prices from their costs and the inflation they expect, not from excess demand. Extra spending raises output, wages (up to a limit) and imports, and demand that cannot be met stays in bank accounts. In a one-seed pilot at 1:1000 scale, paying 10% or 35% of household income a year as new money left the price level within about 8% of the no-payout run over 20 years, while government debt grew without limit. If the study confirms this, the two engines disagree by a factor of hundreds, and the disagreement comes from one assumption each: the engine's quantity-theory rule against BeforeIT's cost-based prices. Neither engine has been shown right about an injection this large (30-50% of income a year), which is far outside anything either was built or tested for. Part 2 reports the figures, whichever way they point.

## 2. The outside engine and the module
- **BeforeIT.jl 0.6.0**, Julia 1.11.5, every package fixed by the committed `Manifest.toml`. Austria, 2010Q1 calibration. Nothing in BeforeIT's package code is edited: the module (`runner/jobs/beforeit/s39.jl`) overloads its social benefit rule and its central bank rule, and acts through its shock hook, the extension routes its documentation shows.
- **Full scale.** The packaged Austria has one agent per 1,000 people. BeforeIT's own calibration code scales a country by multiplying the numbers of people and firms and dividing the amounts per agent (output and wages per worker, benefits per person); the module applies that rule to the packaged country. At 1:1 there are 8.9 million agents; a quarter takes about 100 s on one thread (5.5 GB).
- **One thread.** With several threads BeforeIT does not repeat a seed (its goods market is matched in parallel); on one thread it does (checked). Every run is single-threaded so payout and no-payout runs are paired seed by seed.
- **The payout (Basic Unit and conversion together).** Each quarter: the year's share times the households' nominal income of the previous quarter (payouts included, as the engine's Y includes conversion pay), the same amount to every person, through BeforeIT's per-person benefit, so it is government spending. BeforeIT's households all spend the same share of their income, so who receives it does not change total demand; the BU and conversion pay are therefore one flow here.
- **New money.** The payout adds to the government's deficit. BeforeIT keeps no separate money and bond accounts: the deficit ends as household deposits, the closest it has to the engine's new money.
- **Readings beside it.** The whole payout as new money (gross: none matched by output); the payout financed by an income-tax surcharge; a central bank that answers Austria's own inflation (a national central bank, as in the US) instead of the euro area's; fixed doses of 1%, 3%, 10% and 30% of household income for the pass-through; the Adverse and Stress environments' amounts (only the amounts: BeforeIT's economy stays Austria's); and 1:1000 beside 1:1.
- **Horizon.** 80 quarters (20 years). BeforeIT is built and validated for 12-quarter forecasts; beyond that its no-payout run drifts (in the pilot, unemployment falls to zero after year 5). Both readings are reported; the 20-year one carries this caveat.

## 5. Why BeforeIT's prices do not move: its price rule, read after the runs
This section was written after the results were read, from BeforeIT 0.6.0's source (`src/agent_actions/firms.jl`, `estimations.jl`, `rotw.jl`, `utils/estimate.jl`). The preregistration (check 3) said only that the price rule has no excess-demand term. The rule is narrower than that:
- Each firm sets `P_new = P × (1 + cost push) × (1 + π_e)`. The cost push is the gap between the firm's price and the prices of its inputs: labour at the CPI, materials at the sector prices, capital at the capital-goods price, each weighted by the firm's fixed coefficients.
- The packaged Austria starts every firm's price and every index at 1. Every cost-push term is then zero. All prices grow by the same factor (1 + π_e), so the indices stay equal to each other and the cost push stays zero in every later quarter. Import prices are set to the sector price times (1 + π_e), so they follow too.
- π_e, expected inflation, is a one-lag forecast fitted each quarter to the record of past inflation (the historical quarters plus the model's own), plus a random draw with the fitted residual spread. The model's own inflation is π_e. So the price level follows its own forecast. The payout, demand, wages, deposits and the central bank's rate never enter it.
- The wage term in the cost push uses each firm's normal wage per unit of normal output (`w_bar_i / alpha_bar_i`). When demand exceeds capacity, firms raise output per worker up to 1.5 times and pay wages up to 1.5 times (`w_i`), but the cost push does not read `w_i`. This is how real output rises about 15% at zero unemployment without any effect on prices. Part 1 and the preregistration said extra demand raises wages "through the capacity term". That is right, but the higher wages do not pass into prices.
- **Checked in every run:** the CPI's growth each quarter equals π_e to within 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁵ (all 1,365 runs), and the deflator, food, housing and medical indices equal the CPI to within 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁵ (the tool's identity check).
- **So the payout's expected effect on BeforeIT's prices is exactly zero.** The differences in section 4 are the random draws: once the payout changes how many random numbers the model uses (in its markets), the paired runs' π_e draws diverge. The spread of those differences is noise around zero, which is why the intervals are centred on zero and widen with the horizon.

This is a property of BeforeIT as packaged, with uniform starting prices. A calibration whose starting prices differ by sector, or a price rule that reads demand or actual wages, would behave differently. Neither is BeforeIT's published model, and building one would mean writing a new outside engine. That is outside this step and would no longer be an independent check.

## 6. The decision rules applied
1. **Agreement (rule 1).** The 95% interval of λ for C2.2 Reference, full scale, quarter 80 is -0.012 to 0.010. Its upper end is below 0.25, so by the preregistered rule **the outside engine's prices rise far less than the engine's**. After 20 years the engine's price level is about 408 times its start and BeforeIT's about 1.00 times its own no-payout level (-7% to +7%): a factor of about 400. After 3 years: 2.59 against 1.015 (-0.3% to +3.2%).
2. **What it means (rule 2).** Neither figure shows that the other engine is wrong. The engine's figure rests on its quantity-theory placeholder (`lamG` = 1: every dollar of new money not matched by output reaches the general price level a year later). BeforeIT's rests on its expectations-only price rule, under which, with the packaged uniform starting prices, no spending of any size can move prices (section 5). The cross-check therefore marks out the range the price rule decides, 0 to 1 in pass-through, and about 1 to 400 times in the price level after 20 years under C2.2. It does not narrow it. In BeforeIT the new money shows up as more output (real GDP +15.5% after 3 years, mostly from running each worker's output up to 1.5 times), zero unemployment, and government debt growing without limit (9.8 years of output after 20 years, against 1.1 without the payout). Paid from taxes instead, almost none of the output gain remains.
3. **Horizon (rule 3).** Quarter 12 is inside BeforeIT's validated forecasting range; quarter 80 is not. Its no-payout run drifts: unemployment falls to zero after about year 5, and the CPI reaches 1.31. Every 20-year BeforeIT figure here carries that caveat. The main finding (λ = 0) does not depend on the horizon, because it follows from the price rule.
4. **Consequences (rule 4).** The frozen engine and its published figures do not change. Because rule 1 gives "far less", S39 files a ledger decision for Duke: every Hub sentence on the programme's inflation says the figure rests on the engine's quantity-theory placeholder, and gives the outside engine's figure beside it. Section 5 adds a condition. The outside figure must be given with its reason (BeforeIT's prices follow expected inflation and cannot answer demand), never as "an outside model found no inflation". Suggested wording, for S25 and S40: "The engine's price figures rest on a placeholder rule that passes all new money not matched by output into prices. A published outside model of a whole economy (BeforeIT.jl, calibrated to Austria), given the same new money, shows no price rise, because its prices follow expected inflation and do not respond to demand. In it the money instead raises output (about 15% in three years) and government debt (about ten years of output after twenty years). How much of the money would reach prices is not known from either model." Two items go to the next engine round as open engine questions, with no change now: (a) the price rule's pass-through, which needs an outside source for large money-financed transfers (field and historical evidence, opened and cited) or a price rule that answers demand and capacity; until then `lamG` stays a labelled design parameter, swept 0.25-1. (b) The conversion tax the Source keeps, which is not netted from the unmatched money U (section 1).
5. **Secondary contrasts (rule 5).** With intervals in 4.3. The differences whose intervals exclude zero are all in output or debt, never in prices, apart from the one chance CPI result (dose 3%, full scale, quarter 12). Tax financing removes most of the output gain (Q5). Counting the whole payout as new money adds about 0.6-0.8% to output at 1:1000 (Q4). The output response is 1-2 points larger at full scale in some readings (Q8). The domestic closure changes nothing (Q6). Q7 cannot be compared.

**Correction note (Nov 9, 2026, step S56, after two outside reviews).** Item 2 says the cross-check "marks out the range the price rule decides, 0 to 1 in pass-through". That is the range between the two engines' rules, not a bound on the real pass-through. ## 7. What a US calibration would take
BeforeIT's calibration (`get_params_and_initial_conditions`) reads a country's quarterly national accounts, a 62-industry input-output table, industry output, wages, employment, firm counts and fixed assets, the household, firm, bank and government balance sheets, government spending and benefits by type, and a "rest of the world" (for Austria, the euro area, whose central bank sets Austria's interest rate). For the United States:
- **Data, all public:** BEA's quarterly national accounts and price indexes; BEA's input-output Use and Make tables (71 industries, mapped to BeforeIT's 62 or the model re-dimensioned; FIGARO, which BeforeIT uses for Europe, also has a US table); BEA GDP by industry (output, compensation, employment) and fixed assets; Census SUSB or BDS for firm counts by industry; BLS for unemployment and the inactive population; the Federal Reserve's Financial Accounts (Z.1) for deposits and loans of households, firms and banks; BEA and Treasury government accounts for Social Security, unemployment insurance and other transfers, debt and interest; FRED for the policy rate. The calibration package CalibrateBeforeIT.jl does this for the 27 EU countries from Eurostat; a US version means writing the same mapping from US sources.
- **Model changes:** the US is not a small economy inside a currency union. The rest of the world becomes a world aggregate, and the central bank must answer US inflation and growth (this study's domestic closure is that rule). Imports are a smaller share than Austria's, so less of any demand leaks abroad.
- **Scale:** about 335 million people and 6 million employer firms. At about 0.6 KB per agent, full scale needs about 200 GB of memory, beyond GitHub's free runners (16 GB). Options: run at 1:20 or less (BeforeIT's own calibrations use 1:1000 and 1:2000; Q8 of this study measures what scale changes), or a large-memory machine, which costs money (Runpod: a ledger decision).
- **Validation:** BeforeIT's forecasting record is for Austria and Italy. A US calibration would need its own out-of-sample check before its figures could stand beside the engine's.
- **Effort:** the data mapping and checks are about three to five sessions; the runs at 1:20 fit the free runners.
- **What it would not change:** BeforeIT's price rule. A US calibration changes the size of the leakages (imports) and the central bank's answer; it does not give BeforeIT's firms a price response to excess demand. Whether that response exists at the engine's scale of new money is the real open question, and no calibration of either engine settles it.

## 8. Limits
- The two engines model different economies (the US through 500 to 50,000 adults; Austria through every person and firm). The match is the size of the new money relative to household income, year by year; nothing else is matched.
- The payout is far outside both engines' tested range (30-50% of income a year as new money).
- BeforeIT has no poverty measure; this step compares prices only.
- BeforeIT's no-payout run drifts beyond 12 quarters.
- BeforeIT's price result follows from its price rule and the packaged uniform starting prices (section 5); it is not evidence about how prices respond to new money.
- Full scale has 5 seeds per scenario, so its intervals are wide (about ±7% for the CPI after 20 years); the 1:1000 runs (100 seeds) give the same answer with narrower intervals.
- Section 5 and the identity check were written after the results were read; the preregistered tables (sections 4.1 and 4.2) are exactly as the part-1 tool wrote them, and the rule-5 intervals use the preregistered methods.
